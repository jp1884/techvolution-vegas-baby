## Preface

To most people, corner stores house quick conveniences. To kids, they are wonderlands of comics and candy, magical spaces where imagination and sweetness live in harmony.

I loved visiting my local store. I would casually browse the options, select a comic and treat, and happily hand over cash to the kind shop owner. A warm slice from the pizzeria next door always completed the perfect day.

But inside that place lived a super-villain beyond any comic book.

I would stare at it through the giant "PIZZA" letters on the storefront. Walking back and forth I hoped I would overcome my nemesis. My biggest hope was that only one kind of slice would be available. That way I could bypass the tyrant and get the slice I so wanted.

Was the villain poverty? Meekness? Or perhaps the pizza cook disliked my religion or family? The answer is none of these. The truth is far more fundamental and reveals the core message of the book.

I wanted a slice of pizza. Getting it required speaking, and I did not have the ability to do that, as I was born with a speech impediment—a condition affecting about three million Americans from all walks of life. That is why I turned away when the pizza counter had several kinds of slices. I feared, far more than going hungry, being asked "which one?"

The affliction kicks in when certain sounds trigger a stutter that feels like an electric shock whose severity depends on how hard I keep trying. Speaking to strangers makes it worse. Swearing offers blessed relief, but that solution only works among friends.

Modern science has illuminated many of our biological conditions, providing detailed explanations and effective remedies. In that vein, I was very lucky to receive government-sponsored speech therapy. So although I was subject to a genetic misfire between thought and speech, I learned there was a field that had studied the problem and could tell me what I could do about it. Turns out I was not a broken person, just someone with a broken tool.

Maria, the DVD distribution manager, had no such field. Nobody had studied what was happening to her, so nobody could name it, and she met it without warning. I was given a path, teachings, therapy, and finally a graduation ceremony at an Italian restaurant.

I got to order anything I wanted. It was pizza, of course.

The point of this story is that when a person confronts a problem today—from buying products to getting a job—we instinctively start with a genetic, cultural, or ethnic identity. Listen to any political speech; it will speak to "single mothers," "elderly voters," "new immigrants," or some other demographic group. I do not want to invalidate these identities, only to clarify something fundamental: in the realm of politics and economics, we are first and foremost users of tools.

Some of these tools are biological, like jogging or speaking, but with the explosion of electronics, software, and so many other devices, most of our interactions with the world involve at least some technology. Therefore, our primary identities should be defined by the tools at our disposal, and acquiring better tools to live happier lives ought to be our first priority.

Yet this perspective is almost entirely absent from public discourse—a technological identity is precisely what ideologies strip away from us.

For example, an ideologue seeing a hungry kid outside a pizzeria window might attribute the kid's situation to a personal failing—being a freeloader or wasteful with money—or systemic injustices—like family poverty or discrimination by the shop owner. Both perspectives are possible, but the fact that the kid might be struggling with a "verbal wheelchair" rarely occurs to people today. This oversight happens not because they are unaware of disabilities, but because ideological interpretations are often our default lens when seeing problems in society.

That should not be too surprising. Our ideologies have guided our mindsets for generations. Given the massive technological ecosystem we now enjoy, they have done an admirable job. Yet conservatism (1790) and progressivism (1859) were conceived centuries ago. The First Industrial Revolution (1760–1840) brought steam power and textile mills. The Second (1870–1914) brought electricity, mass production, and the assembly line. With each decade the established craft guilds, manorial farms, royal palaces were replaced with wage labor, mass migration, and urban poverty. Our current ideologies were born because existing political and economic frameworks were unable to navigate the new terrain of industrialization.

Conservative and progressive thinkers offer valuable insights into our societal policies and governance, but their worldview is not rooted in the modern scientific and technological world they helped create. They lived without plastic, wrote with bird feathers, and did not learn how evolution shaped the state of nature. Ardent believers in one of these ideologies today are stuck solving the problems of the distant past. Anyone who studies both can build on their shared achievements.

Conservatism emphasizes producing wealth through mass production. Progressivism pushes for social diversity to empower more individuals. Viewed through a technological identity, these mindsets work best together.

Every biological ecosystem is a mixture of different lifeforms harvesting energy to mass-produce as much growth and offspring as possible. But life also varies its offspring, searching for any that suit the latest environment better. Conservatism describes how life populates its ecosystems; progressivism describes how it updates them. Together they populated the world with many different engines, factories, power stations, and other industrial wonders. Thus, rather than seeing left versus right as opposing forces, we can view them as complementary perspectives contributing to our collective technological evolution.

Sharing the mission of industrialization, the two sides often found common ground. In the 1950s, Republicans and Democrats could be so intermingled in their voting and collaboration that they sometimes didn't look like two separate parties. Even through the turbulent 1960s and early 1970s—with the fierce battles over the Great Society programs, the Vietnam War, and the Civil Rights movement raging—Republicans and Democrats regularly voted together. Cross-party cooperation wasn't rare or exceptional. It was the norm, with about 12,000 individual Republican-Democrat voting partnerships taking place in the House of Representatives.

Neither side gave in. The right pushed to keep building more structures, roads, and power plants. The left made sure ever more disenfranchised people got in, so they could work, contribute, and share in the spoils of industrialization. The moderates in both parties held the power, and they often compromised to their mutual benefit. Graphs of party voting behavior in this era look like a tossed salad.

But from the mid-1970s onward, that cooperation began steadily eroding. By the 1990s, the two parties had pulled into distinct and largely separate camps. By the 2000s, cross-party cooperation had collapsed almost entirely with fewer than 200 cooperating pairs voting across party lines in the House of Representatives. Surprisingly, the further we've moved from the origins of these ideologies, the more extreme our partisanship has become. The party voting graphs now look like oil and water.

Unsurprisingly, our economic challenges continue to mount.

Today's statistics paint a stark picture of our financial strain. Adjusted to 2023 dollars, median household income rose from about $62,000 in 1991 to about $79,000 in 2021, an increase of roughly 28 percent over thirty years. The cost of living has dramatically outpaced this increase.

Average home prices across U.S. states were about 345 percent higher in 2024 than in 1991. Average annual premiums for employer-sponsored family health coverage increased 49 percent from 2013 to 2023. Average in-state tuition roughly doubled from 1991 to 2023. Grocery costs rose significantly in recent years—a 20–30 percent increase from 2020 to 2025.

This widening economic gap has driven debt to record levels for students and governments. American federal debt, measured against the size of the economy, is now near its 1946 peak. That should strike us as strange. Much of what World War II (1939-1945) wartime debt bought was destroyed within months, yet by the mid-1970s the debt had shrunk to about a quarter of its postwar size relative to the economy because a healthy economy outgrew its debt. Today’s federal borrowing comes not from war but from the ordinary costs of keeping society running: retirement, commuting, definitely insurance, and the interest on what we already owe. Just keeping our society running runs up as much debt as a world war, which suggests something has gone profoundly wrong.

Economic betterment has also largely stopped in America, with only half of Americans born in 1980 earning more than their parents, compared to 90 percent of those born in 1940.

I don't need to cite any more numbers because you see this trend every month when matching your paycheck to your bills. This demands a question: the internet and personal computer were both invented in the early 1970s, right as our politics started to divide and our economy began to stall. Despite computers growing ever more powerful, taking us further from the Industrial Age into the Internet Age, many people are falling behind. Why?

Just as religious frameworks fell out of date during early industrialization, our ideological ones are out of date now. Out of touch, they can’t offer a shared purpose. Out of touch, they can't offer a shared purpose, and so cannot mass-produce or adopt new tools in our new computer-powered environment

Industrial Age mindsets are simply not up for the task. Ideologues, pundits, and believers say their superior government policy can address our challenges. As each has proven ineffective we're told to blame the "other side". Growing extremism hasn't worked because evolution demands more: we must adapt by innovating new tools and adopting a new lifestyle.

Before we do that we must adopt a fresh mindset. One built from the lessons conservatives and progressives taught us when they conquered the Industrial Age. Consider a simple experiment: combine cold water and ice into a bucket. As you immerse your hand in the water, the cold steals your heat, and your body soon experiences intense pain. This visceral moment reveals a fundamental truth: no words, presentations, or promises keep you warm.

Obviously not. Words don't fill our stomachs or shelter us from the elements.

Yet today we evaluate conservatism and progressivism by their words. Our ancestors didn't do that. Instead, they valued each for their ability to create and distribute tools like farm tractors, home heaters, and community libraries. These are adaptations that overcame real-life pains of hunger, poverty, illiteracy. Today, ideological believers, from bureaucrats to teachers, executives to workers, say they promote adopting innovative computer technology, but only when you adopt a technological identity will you evaluate their words by how well the tools in your hands solve your frontline problems.

Using Industrial Age mentalities in the Internet Age is a massive mix-up. It interferes with matching our problems with better tools. When we realize this, we can once again mass-produce the diverse tools we need to find a shared political purpose and build economic prosperity again.

Then we'll be following evolution, and that is a good place to be. Life has spent billions of years combating heat, hunger, thirst, and cold through new adaptations. Arctic fish develop antifreeze proteins. Polar birds grow waterproof feathers. Mammals develop fur and blubber. Each organism's survival depends not on avoiding the discomforts of hot or cold, or assigning blame for them, but on developing the right tools to keep pain away in its environment.

Our ancestors lost their insulating hair when they left the trees and learned to walk. How did we stay warm? We cut fur from dead animals and mastered fire. Today, we synthesize materials, generate heat, and create any other solution we can dream up. This enormous technological progress has often reduced our pains, but that also means we easily forget what life on Earth is all about.

Hence, we tend to view the world through human stories, speeches, and ideologies, rather than the reality that we need to address practical problems with new adaptations. This ironically causes frontline pains to increase.

Adopting a strong technological identity will keep our continued evolution in focus. A new identity does not mean abandoning any of our wonderful cultures. We can still enjoy music clubs, celebrate folklore dances, commemorate history, and cheer at the Olympics. However, in a world increasingly shaped by technological systems, we must develop a new mindset to clearly identify our problems and see their best solutions.

The everyday lesson is, loving the same music as someone else is great fun, talking about ideology can cause interesting debates, but neither means you have found the best mechanic. Solving our frontline problems means finding better tools and better tool-users. Just think how an oak tree and a squirrel have nothing in common, and each helps the other prosper every year.

All lifeforms should work together because Earth is a massive version of an ice bucket—a vast game board shaped by immutable laws of physics, chemistry, and geology. Life produces new creatures constantly, and its partner, evolution, decides which keep playing. With the right team, Earth is an awe-inspiring oasis—a lone lifeboat in the galactic expanse.

The story of life now involves technology. Its many gadgets, tools, and inventions are already changing the world. The purpose of this book is to help you realize tools are the best way to adapt to your environment. A recurring theme is how to combat any story that tries to subvert this evolutionary requirement.

Our beliefs should lift us to new heights. They should tell us our limitations do not define us. A limitation is just a problem, and problems yield to better tools and better allies. I learned that at a pizza counter, one pointed finger, one slice, and one slow word at a time. This book is about learning it at the scale of a civilization.

## Introduction 

### Catching Life's Eye

Rousseau famously wrote that humans are born free, and everywhere we are in chains. The opposite is closer to the truth for the rest of nature: animals are born free, and everywhere they seem to be enjoying themselves.

Nature is no doubt a tough place to be. Hunger, disease, thirst, and predation are always just around the corner. Yet, from the mighty grizzly bear to the tiny firefly, organisms fight hard to stay alive. In their own way, creatures seem to like playing the "Game of Life".

So, how does a life form get selected to become a player? There are three ways to get selected: genes, personality, and tools.

Genes are a creature’s blueprint, and taken together they make up its genotype. Each stretch of DNA carries instructions for a particular trait. When creatures reproduce, their genes shuffle and recombine, and sometimes there's a copying error that adds something new. Because of these mutations every offspring is slightly different. Most of these mutations are neutral or minor, but now and then one gives a survival advantage.

Consider the peppered moth for an example. During England's Industrial Revolution trees darkened due to smog and soot. A rare dark variant of the peppered moth suddenly gained a camouflage advantage. While its competitors were hunted down and the dark mutate offspring surged. Evolution did not create this mutation; rather, it favored the black moth through natural selection—a process that eliminates harmful genes while allowing organisms with beneficial traits to survive and reproduce.

This continuous process of genetic variation and environmental selection drives the incremental changes we observe in species over time—sometimes slowly, as geological change creates mountains or freezes oceans over eons, or suddenly with events like floods, eruptions, or factory soot. Regardless, as the environment changes, natural selection can only work with the life that already exists.

That's why diversity is so important. It's what made the dark moth variant that let the species still prosper. But diversity needs a balance: without mass numbers, what difference could oddballs make?

This is the pairing of two opposing halves that we saw described above in political terms. It is not a metaphor. It is how natural selection works. That's why, over time, species and ecosystems with greater genetic diversity persist only if they can reproduce in numbers. Too much of either is a failure: genetic variety with no volume goes nowhere, volume with no variety has nothing to offer when the trees turn black. A successful genotype needs both the oddball and the crowd.

The next way a creature can stand out is its phenotype. The phenotype is akin to a life form's personality; the observable characteristics of an organism resulting from the interaction between its genetic makeup and environmental influences, encompassing physical traits, behavioral adaptations, and learned experiences. Think of two acorns, one planted in a tall forest, the other in an open field. The first grows lean and tall to reach sunlight, while the other grows bushier and more robust to withstand harsh winds. Same genes, different forms.

Phenotype reveals how organisms adapt to their surroundings and become products of their environment.

Phenotypic adaptations show remarkable diversity. Some changes are enabled through genetics and are immediate, like a cuttlefish changing its skin to match the coral patterns; others are learned and passed down socially, like an elephant mother showing her calf where the water holes are. Like with genes, traits are better or worse only relative to their performance in the environment. Natural selection favors whichever phenotype passes on its genes more than others.

The third way creatures stand out is the extended phenotype. These are adaptations that creatures build for themselves. Think of bird nests, beaver dams, and ant hills. Although made of foreign material, these things act like any other organ by helping a creature better survive.

Take the common squirrel as an example. The small creature lives in the forests and eats tree nuts. It survives frigid winters because it stores nuts during good times and builds warm nests high in the canopy away from predators. These adaptations greatly increase its range and survival rate. Extended phenotypes empower organisms to mold their environment to their benefit and thereby select themselves for survival.

Unlike genes and phenotypes, extended phenotypes become part of the environment and are available for other creatures to use. Hermit crabs move into empty snail shells, fish and frogs fill the ponds that beavers dammed, and squirrel's uneaten nuts are tomorrow's forest. The creator of an extended phenotype is not always its user.

Raccoons are the ultimate example. Originally from the tropical forests of Central America and Mexico, these intelligent creatures create dens in hollow trees and grow thicker fur and fat stores for winter. These adaptations let them slowly increase their range northward into colder parts of America. However, unlike squirrels, raccoons do not insulate their dens, store food, or fully hibernate.

But when human barns and garbage arrived, the raccoon's range exploded. Piggybacking on civilization these creatures thrived. Many urbanites around the world know this all too well. It's why our garbage bins must be raccoon-proofed. Or at least try to be, as raccoons often outwit us.

We learn something about extended phenotypes by contrasting raccoons with squirrels. A squirrel takes what it finds, but it renews the biological ecosystem it feeds on by planting many nuts each year. An urban raccoon gets food and shelter but renews nothing it depends on. Because extended phenotypes sit outside the body, whoever uses them doesn’t have to earn it, or give anything back to the ecosystem that made it. In nature, the squirrel can’t feed itself without planting the forest. Our technological ecosystem has no such natural requirement.

So, to stay a player in the Game of Life, creatures can stand out via their genes, phenotypes, and extended phenotypes. Having the right mixture of all three means an animal can harvest enough resources and protect itself long enough to procreate and thereby keep playing the game.

Now, in our quest to better understand ourselves, which of these characteristics do you think best describes humans in the 21st century?

### Doesn't My Insurance Cover This?

The answer is the raccoon. Like them, we live, work, and exist in an ecosystem of tools without knowing how they work. This is why the thesis of this book is that humans are now primarily a technological species that is not yet self-aware of our new identity.

In evolutionary terms, Maria wasn't a "regional manager" at all—that's a cultural label. She was a physical-media seller dependent on several adaptations fitted to one niche, destined to rise and fall with it. That's what a species is. And around 2011, the environment changed, so her niche was going extinct, whatever she or others called herself.

Maria wasn't alone. _Home Improvement_ was one of America's most popular television shows in the 1990s. Week after week, tens of millions of families watched Tim "The Tool Man" Taylor host "Tool Time"; a show only shown on Detroit cable television that reviewed tools from his sponsor Binford Tools. Tim's famous phrase was "More power!" because he constantly over-engineered tools and projects, leading to spectacular (and often disastrous) results that the competent, mellow co-host Al must deal with.

_Home Improvement_ was culturally aware enough to tackle the difficulties of drug addiction, infidelity, and job loss. Yet in eight seasons, technological identity never appeared on that list. Nobody, not even the Tool Man, asked what was powering his own show. He never asked what happens when technology, like telecommunications, changes and gets more powerful.

Meanwhile internet speeds were improving every year. In 1992 when the show premiered, it was 0.0144 Mbps and loading simple websites took minutes. By 1999, when the show ended, it was 0.056 Mbps and online gaming was possible. Soon, the same cable networks showing Tool Time, were converted to high-speed internet. By 2011 internet speeds were 31 Mbps and video production and distribution were increasingly streaming on YouTube, Netflix, and other platforms.

How could regional cable television shows—or DVD rental stores—not be changed forever by more powerful internet speeds?

Today people ask anxiously whether AI will take their jobs—a frightening question precisely because so few of us understand our relationship to the technologies we depend on to earn a living. Even while consuming better and more powerful home products every year. Anyone with a smartphone has a better supercomputer, video camera, wireless connection, than anyone in the 1990s. Never mind easy access to encyclopedia, product manuals, and video instructions Tim Taylor never had. We should be celebrating that like another Moon landing.

We don't.

Because like Maria, we have no name for that part of our identity—the extended phenotype that selection acts on most often. My own story shows why this blindspot is so hard to see.

Among my genetic traits is the already mentioned speech impediment. Centuries ago a stutterer was taken for a simpleton and a candidate for neglect or worse. I can sympathize; in childhood I was thought to be one because I rarely talked. But the problem was identified at my elementary school, a therapist was assigned, and the shackles of my genetic heritage began to come off.

The resulting shyness was harder. This part of my phenotype is like most people's story, with kids unconsciously adapting to their social environment as best they can. Years later a software company told me the impediment was still noticeable, so I resolved to fix it in a notoriously chaotic insurance claims call center. Within a year that sink-or-swim therapy had worked out most of my speaking tool's glitches.

This is not unique. Many people face social anxiety, dyslexia, epilepsy. And we have numerous ways to address these issues. Science has mapped the human genome and offers genetic help while psychology's many therapies provide significant support. Human genotype and phenotype are well studied. Because of our mastery, selection has far less reason to remove us for having any of these traits.

As for the extended phenotype, before the Industrial Revolution, we were often like squirrels using tools made from the natural world. Technology still amazed us, but things such as sod houses, wood saws, and paper books are physical objects easy to hold, safe to smell, and natural to the touch. However, after the massive explosion of chemicals, materials, electricity, electronics, software, and so many other things over the past 150 years, we are now like raccoons who depend on things we do not truly understand.

I know this in my soul. After my speech improved as a claims adjuster, I looked around and realized how valuable the job was. I processed more than 7,000 claims and spoke with more than 30,000 people from all walks of life. By getting into people's shoes when their furnaces died, cars crashed, or houses flooded, the job puts you at the pain center of the economy.

For a political economy student being a claims adjuster was like living a daily experiment. It was an intellectual gold mine.

The ultimate lesson was how many people struggle to understand modern technologies. We live in a world of increasing complexity but diminishing comprehension. From wealthy executives to blue-collar workers, everyone is equally perplexed by a car crash or a malfunctioning geo-thermal furnace. People rarely understand their own possessions or the rules governing their use, always one glitch away from asking, "Doesn't my insurance cover this?" to a person on the phone as if I had a magic wand to erase their problems.

I was so often a customer's best hope, yet I had no special tools but a stingy checkbook and Google search.

Modern technology is a massive explosion of extended phenotypes, and has changed how evolution selects us. Comparing top causes of death in modern America and ancient societies (circa 500 BC) reveals our new selection pressure. Modern America's list is dominated by heart disease, diabetes, and chronic diseases linked to our sedentary lifestyle that is so pervasive that raccoons suffer from many of the same problems. In contrast, in ancient times our ancestors suffered greatly from infectious diseases and injuries due to poor sanitation, limited medical care, and frequent wars. Notably, infant mortality has dropped dramatically, from perhaps as high as 40% in the Bronze Age to under 1% in America today.

Like the Tool Man, we do not naturally evolve with all these tools.

Adjusters work with appraisers who help evaluate car and property damages. One I worked with measured large commercial buildings with a manual measuring wheel, taking days to do what a laser measuring tool does in hours. A CEO banned smartphones for adjusters and insisted on landlines and face to face visits, long after customers had moved on to faster ways of reaching us. Neither man was a villain. Both wanted to protect the tools they'd mastered—which is exactly the point. Adaptation can stall without any bad intent—through the ordinary, understandable instinct to defend one's territory.

Because extended phenotypes are separate from our bodies, they evolve on different timescales. As technological innovation speeds up, we are easily left behind. To solve our problems with upgraded tools, we need to see ourselves not just as people who use tools, but as first and foremost "Tool people".

Consider this: what do you find more concerning—spraining an ankle or having water pipes burst while on vacation?

Our ancestors might be eaten by a lion or left for dead because of a physical injury, but today we would simply rest and watch Netflix. However, the flood from a burst water pipe could bankrupt you. I should know; I had to deny claims because homeowners broke the unknown conditions of their insurance policies. If they had been technologically self-aware enough to perform a simple two-minute water shut-off, they could have prevented years of hardship and kept life's torch well-lit.

Yet, we rarely examine the extended phenotype part of ourselves. We remain barely conscious of our technological identities and fail to proactively develop skills that would satisfy evolutionary pressures. Far more people define themselves by gender, race, or heritage, compared to the tools they use to gather resources. Technological identity teaches the raccoon what a restaurant is, why it's not very busy for five nights, and maybe even how to work the dishwasher. 

Once we strip away labels like television host, regional manager, even “raccoon,” we see what each one actually does in its ecosystem. The raccoon is a scavenger living off our leftovers. Tim was a local television star in a niche millions of YouTubers would soon fill. Maria was a distributor whose pivotal tool was getting left behind. Tim’s and Maria’s jobs were challenged by high-speed internet the same way a new locking waste bin challenges a raccoon.

Seeing that is when “More power!” becomes real power.

But left unaware of modern tools, we resemble raccoons opportunistically using technology we encounter, believing things like genetics, education, or ideological stories explain human success or failure. As evolution penalizes our ignorance, we must look in the mirror and be honest about what we see: people who passionately discuss human genes and/or personality, but are in reality fighting to control tools, even ones as simple as a pencil.

### Avoiding Evolution's Stare

Charles Darwin didn’t invent the idea that creatures evolve. What he and Alfred Russel Wallace proposed in 1858 was the mechanism: natural selection. Darwin’s _On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection_ followed a year later, built on decades of observation, and it faced two obstacles. The first was time. Natural selection needed an enormously old Earth, but some religious figures thought the Earth was 6,000 years old, while leading scientists said millions. Not until radioactive dating (1906) was discovered did scientists prove Earth was billions of years old. Natural selection now had the time it needed to work its—seeming—magic.

The second obstacle was harder to answer. Nobody understood how creatures inherit traits. The prevailing assumption was that offspring simply blend their parents' features. So a strong mother and weak father make a moderately strong offspring. This raised an obvious objection. If mating means losing half of any advantage every generation, how could an advantage like larger wings or faster muscles ever accumulate enough for natural selection to act on it?

That question went unanswered for seventy years. Naturalists accepted Darwin's idea that animals had a common ancestry because they'd been cataloguing and classifying organisms by shared characteristics for over a century, making shared connections easy enough to see.

Still, few accepted the mechanism for new species was natural selection. Rival theories crowded biology with possible answers, the main one being Lamarckism: that species learn the requirements of their environments and pass on those traits. A giraffe stretches its neck for leaves and thus its children have longer necks. With countless books, speeches, and lectures raging on, Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909 coined the terms genotype and phenotype to help frame the debate with a consistent vocabulary.

Johannsen’s words named the confusion at the heart of the debate. Scientists were mixing up what a creature inherits with what it learns or its environment shapes. Remember the two acorns in a forest and meadow have the same genes, but they still exist in different forms. Biology needed two distinct words, genotype and phenotype, to see that selection judges a creature by its phenotype, but only its genotype is passed on. The debate wasn’t fully settled until the 1940s.

Meanwhile physics and chemistry were giving us electricity, medicine, and flight. So why didn’t we apply evolution’s principles to advance civilization?

We attempted to. But we only saw our murky understanding of genotype or phenotype in the reflection, and were therefore destined to fail. Remember that evolution does not prescribe good ideas; it selects out the bad ones. Two of the worst ideas ever subjected to that test arose while scientists were still arguing how new species are created in the early 1900s.

These were fascism and communism.

One looked at us and saw only genotype: each person is solely defined by their bloodline, so act accordingly—promote the supermen and purge the dead weight with human breeding called eugenics. The other saw only phenotype and said we must empower educated decision makers to engineer society's education, work, laws, government, to remake the human condition.

Of course, the mapping isn’t perfect. Neither regime used these terms exactly, and both had other influences. But fascism built its identity on bloodline, heritage, and the individually gifted, all genotype’s terms, while communism set out to remake people by remaking their environment, education, and morals, all phenotype’s realm.

Both thought their way would create the most powerful modern technologies.

The timing matters. Conservatism and progressivism arose in the 19th century, in relatively poor societies struggling with early industrialization. They wanted to pave mud roads and learn to make lots of shoes. Fascism and communism took power in the 20th, when the factories and power stations already existed, but the horrors of World War I and the failures of the Great Depression made people believe the old mindsets didn’t know how to use modern technology. Looking for a new mindset, people turned to science. Because what made natural selection work was still unsettled, each extreme could bend it to its own premise: that focusing on genotype alone, or phenotype alone, was humanity’s path to prosperity.

However, as awful as it might sound, the problem isn't that an extreme couldn't have a technological success. Each was doomed because neither could sustain any success. We'll explain in detail in a later chapter, but in short, extremes have complacency embedded in them because each disregards one half of evolution.

This was proven in biology too late. Around 1918, mathematicians showed that genes don’t blend, they shuffle, so advantages like bigger wings can survive over time, just as Darwin’s theory needed. By the 1940s the Modern Synthesis showed how genotype and phenotype work together through co-evolution, variation, and speciation to produce life’s astonishing diversity. Sadly, by then, the fascist and communist regimes were already distorting the 20th century.

Defining human success by genotype alone, or phenotype alone, is a dead end. Nature doesn’t sort itself that way. A tree’s genes give it options, a tall thin trunk or a short bushy one, and its surroundings decide which is needed. Good genes are great but they are basically a catalogue; a good form is great but is limited to what the genes provide. Just as nature needs both diversity and mass production, it needs both genotype and phenotype to procreate into the future.

This is vital because a tree that grows does more than survive. It shades the ground, shelters nests, holds the soil, and feeds the squirrels that plant its offspring. None of that is the tree’s intention. In nature nobody sets out to help anybody but the forest happens anyway, and everyone benefits.

It must be asked: if even the extremists wanted the best new technologies, why was it so difficult for humanity to recognize ourselves as technological beings? The reason is the same as why accepting evolution took so long: it contradicts the paradigms entrenched in our minds. Like all lifeforms, competing in the Game of Life is in our nature. And what better strategy for biological survival than possessing a tool that others lack, or better yet, need?

We want to invent new technologies but don't think to spread them.

To illustrate, in the aftermath of fascist (and more broadly communist) atrocities, psychologist Stanley Milgram set out to understand how ordinary people had participated in such events. At Yale University in 1961, participants acted as "teachers" giving a memory test to a "learner" (an actor). After each error, the teacher was to inflict an increasingly strong electric shock. The learner responded in more pain while an authoritative scientist with a clipboard and pencil would insist politely that the experiment continue.

In Milgram's most-cited setup, 65 percent of teachers flipped the switch on what they believed were dangerous shocks. Many showed visible distress but continued with the test anyway. The experiment has been recreated and the share of teachers giving shocks has varied, but the pattern has held: ordinary people under perceived authority often comply despite the pain they cause.

Perhaps because of my speech impediment, watching these experiments highlighted a simple truth: we should never judge one another's raw capabilities. To me, the Milgram setup is not primarily about humanity's willingness to follow orders but about our limited awareness and misguided self-identity.

Despite having pencils and paper sitting on the table, no teacher ever thought to offer them to the learner to help him remember better.

Humans need tools. After all, the scientist used a pencil to record test results. Yet here we were inflicting pain on each other instead of simply offering a pencil to a person in need. This is not just bad for the learner or teacher. It's bad for everyone.

Neither great genes nor elite education can match what a good toolbox does. Our success depends on enhancing capabilities through technology, and like the squirrel and oak tree, we rely on others' technological empowerment to sustain the technological ecosystem we depend on.

There is a reason why conservatives and progressives outlasted both fascism and communism. When they transitioned into the early Industrial Age, they granted some basic protections for the tools of this era. The freedom from religious domination, the right to use a printing press, own a gun, be protected from search or seizure are all freedoms to use modern technology.

These simple protections beat both extremes because they let everyone keep a pencil, and that keeps variety alive. When England cleaned its air and the bark turned pale again, the rare pale moths were still there to be selected, and the species carried on.

We'll never know who might be the next "black moth" that saves us.

For now, stuck using Industrial Age mentalities in the Internet Age, we invent technology but do not naturally distribute it to others. This shortcoming appears in various contexts: workplaces where employees receive tools without input yet are judged on their performance using the tools; schools that test children’s knowledge and memory while denying common aids like notes or calculators; individuals who neglect their own toolsets, hoping instead that an insurance representative will resolve their issues with a claim check.

These aren't separate grievances. They're the same pattern. In the technological era, a person who can't control her tools is half an organism, and withholding the tools is what selection pressure now looks like.

From Gen Z to retirees, the tools that would earn an independent livelihood sit just out of reach the way the printing press, plot of land, and freedom to choose any mindset once did.

And here is what that produces. When people can't see why their lives are getting harder, they reach for the explanation on offer, which is now to blame the other side. The country thus blames itself for a squeeze no one caused on their own. The Milgram participants had the same problem. Everything they needed was on the table, and not one of them saw it, so they kept doing the only thing they'd been given to do.

The pencil is still on the table. Somebody has been leaving them there for decades.

Because reality has no rulebook to read or program to hack. Nature never cares about our input, ideas, or feedback. Only by investigating the environment can we uncover these rules, to which there is always another layer to master.

The laws of physics, chemistry, geology make the stars and sunlight, hydrogen and helium, planets and volcanoes. As the rulebook makes clear, these Rules of the Game are not written down like a cookbook. Instead they are embedded in the very fabric of existence.

The laws of nature are rather simple but they create endlessly complex results. The land, sea, and sky make our playing field, and the laws of nature constantly remake it.

Consider our Moon—not merely a celestial object that happens to fly across our sky. It is the result of cosmic collision and gravitational interaction. Approximately 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized planetary body called Theia collided with the early Earth.

The Moon, in a sense, is built from Earth's cast-off skin. Gravity pulled these fragments together. Over time, these fragments coalesced, cooling and solidifying into what we now know as the Moon.

Yet, despite its massive size, this collision was controlled by the same natural laws at work as I write these words and you read them.

It causes our ocean's tides, illuminates the night, has mysterious phases, and could be colonized. These opportunities and obstacles in our path transform it from a distant object into a factor in our survival.

Our interaction with the Rules of the Game turns these abstract natural laws into our game board.

That’s what makes the game board emergent. It doesn’t exist until you meet it. No two people stand on the same board: the same tornado can flatten one house and leave its neighbor untouched. No one caused it. There’s no one to blame for the hail or a cold winter night either. They just happen, on everyone’s board.

A recent study suggested that two of Earth’s mass extinctions, the Ordovician about 445 million years ago and the Late Devonian about 372 million years ago, may have been triggered by nearby stars exploding as supernovas. Imagine that.

One of those twinkling lights might be too far away to ever matter. Or it could kill almost everything on Earth and send life back to near the starting line.

The game board is variable. It could be a supernova trillions of kilometers away, peat moss from ancient decaying plants, or it could be the cold of night closing in on a lost traveler who thought he was enjoying a country ride.

In a vast, perhaps infinite universe, the factors that can shape our survival are nearly endless. That’s why our ability to perceive our environment, our game board, matters so much.

## Chapter 4—Sand Clock: The Ever-Present Timer

Have you ever wondered how the game board is made? I mean, volcanoes, stars, everything, is made up of atoms. So how do these countless tiny atoms come together into such unique objects? Does nature have a "make a volcano" button?

No. Mythologies and video games have these shortcuts; reality does not. Instead, to make anything in the universe requires concentrated energy. This useful kind of energy happens when gravity, nuclear forces, and electromagnetism interact with each other and matter. When these natural forces concentrate enough matter, they can transform it, and create pockets of useful energy. 

But forces don't act chaotically. They follow strict rules called natural laws, which govern how energy and matter flow, concentrate, and disperse. Understanding both the forces and the laws is how we understand how nature makes everything.

For example, volcanoes exist when gravity packs rock into a planet’s interior, where leftover heat from its formation and radioactive decay keep it hot. Lighter, molten rock rises, finds weak points in the crust, and erupts as a volcano. In a star, gravity squeezes hydrogen so intensely that nuclear forces fuse it into helium and release immense electromagnetic radiation.

The cycle of natural forces gathering matter, transforming, and releasing energy happens over and over according to the natural laws. Across the entire universe, that’s how planets, stars, continents, and the rest of our game board, down to the atoms themselves, are made. 

But concentrated useful energy only lasts so long. A brilliant volcano’s heat slowly seeps away until it goes dormant and cold. When a mighty star burns through its fuel, it will eventually collapse, explode, or slowly fade. Natural forces organize matter, but over time matter and energy disorganize.

And understanding how objects disarrange is effectively our game clock; just like an hourglass, it tells us how much time our game board has left.

Science calls this the second law of thermodynamics. Its measure is entropy, a quantity that describes nature's one-way tendency: energy disperses, structures break down, and things fade away over time.

In a way, entropy is the other half of the universe’s accounting of energy. If we imagine that gravity and nuclear forces gather energy, we can think of entropy as spending it. Nothing forbids atoms from staying arranged as mountains or sandcastles. But as time passes and wind storms, ocean waves, and car crashes happen, there are vastly more ways for atoms to scatter than stay organized. There are only a few ways for sand to make a castle, but nearly infinite ways to arrange sand on a beach. Anything that stays organized is beating staggering odds but ultimately will lose the fight.

To see what those odds do to a whole planet, compare these two.

Earth and Mars were once about the same. Their cores were hot magma. They had protective magnetospheres. Their atmospheres were thick with gases. Liquid water flowed in rivers as volcanoes shaped the landscape. Unlike some planets Mars and Earth were far from bland or boring. Each had the energy to organize objects into cool physical, chemical and geological features like mountains, lakes, and atmosphere. The forces of nature created two relatively similar planets.

But entropy is always there, so it went to work on both.

Volcanic explosions and freezing water degrade mountains, wind storms turn rock into sands, sunlight dries up wetlands, and after enough heat is lost in the core, magnetospheres end their shielding, and solar radiation expels gases from the atmosphere. All of this turns the planet still and motionless, like Mars is today. Because Mars is much smaller, its core cooled relatively fast, so its magnetosphere failed, causing its atmosphere to be all but washed away. There are little to no volcanic eruptions because there is not enough heat in the core to build up the energy for it.

Mars shows a world of increasing uniformity and less organization. But Earth still has much of everything because our core is still very hot, so it gives us the heat and magnetic protection from the Sun's damaging charged particles. Billions of years from now, the heat will run out, the magnetosphere will die, and the natural forces via wind, rust, and radiation will grind it down. Earth will again be lifeless and dull once again.

And, neither will ever return to their former glory.

That is why entropy is often associated with the arrow of time. Events in the universe can only happen once. Meaning a volcano's explosive eruption will never spontaneously reverse itself, the same way a pot of coffee will never un-mix to its original milk, bean, and sugar parts.

All this means there is no going back. So, every place in the universe is unique because every moment will never be repeated.

Physicists call the board and the clock together spacetime. This term combines the three dimensions of space we saw in Spaceland (length, width, height) with the dimension of time into a single, interconnected four-dimensional continuum.

While the game board can tell you where you are, the game clock says when you exist. Combining the two makes every second you live, whether it's a walk in the park or reading this page, totally unique. The game board and game clock are forever emergent and relative to where and when you exist in the universe.

The arrow of time is what Maria needed to learn. She was surrounded by awards, titles, mind share, and other elements of human mythology. These made it seem the DVD business was solid. The physics said its moment in time was always going to be limited.

Like everything else, the DVD was an arrangement held together by constant work—pressing plants running, trucks moving, shelves restocked, stores staffed, customers visiting. Stop pouring energy into any of it and the whole thing comes apart. Streaming carried the same movie for less work. One example, Blockbuster alone had 60,000 workers with thousands of rental stores. Netflix has about 15,000 workers and runs on multiple contracted huge data centers. Lots of work to create and maintain, but still far less than the DVD. Such is the difference between physical transport versus digital.

In a universe where energy is limited, the thing that takes up less has far better chance of existing into the future.

The game clock runs on every arrangement we build. A coal town is a town because coal is worth digging; the school, the diner, and the hardware store are all downstream of coal's ability to energize a nation. A local newspaper was printing presses, delivery routes, and classified ads that paid for the newsroom, until the lucrative classifieds moved online and the newsroom lost what fed it.

None of these were destroyed in an attack. The energy holding them together either ran out, like a star, or found an easier path, like lava finding a new vent.

Remember the question we started with: who is our common true enemy? Not the other party, the other job, or the other side. It’s the sandclock. Entropy is the one law of nature that puts a timer on everything we build and everyone we love. It can never be reset or sped up. It's what gives our choice its consequence.

That clock is running on your arrangement too. So run the test. What does it cost in energy to keep your lifestyle running: the roadways, the car, the commute, the office, the house? And what would it cost something else, perhaps AI, perhaps something else entirely, to do the same job?

You don't need a prediction. You need the number.

Unlike in chess, our board is always changing under our feet. Time itself is a constraint: organized structures—volcanoes, sandcastles, pyramids, tanks of gas—disperse unless energy is invested to maintain them. We can emulate this game clock with sand clocks or timepieces. Each tells us how much time we have before entropy disperses the order if no work is done to maintain it.

And this reveals the ultimate puzzle: in a universe that disperses the energy forming planets and stars, what kind of thing can work against this tide? What kind of thing can harvest energy faster than entropy removes it?

The answer is something the universe had not produced before—until it did.

## Chapter 5—Clay: What Life Is, The Creator of Players

You twirl the small sand clock in your hand. Sand goes from one side to the other. You realize just how many grains of sand there are, and just how many variations of "sand" there really are.

Once the sand runs out, the clock is useless. Only when someone invests energy to flip it does it tell time again.

Done with the clock, you set it aside on top of the game board and rulebook. Now you pick up the clay and roll it in your hands: smash, stretch, roll, pinch. How does this soft malleable stuff relate to the Game of Life?

First let's go back and emphasize something very important.

Most people know Albert Einstein's work E = mc². This revolutionary equation says "Energy equals mass times the speed of light squared", which when doing the math means even a small amount of matter (mass) stores an enormous amount of energy. More importantly, this equation says in the right circumstance (like inside stars), matter and energy can be converted into each other.

Scientifically there is no direct link between entropy and Einstein's equation, but noting mass and energy equivalency helps us understand a crucial point. As entropy degrades objects into uniformity, energy is never created or destroyed, but just moved around into different states of radiation (light) or matter.

Basically, there is no delete button anywhere in physics—energy and matter are never destroyed, only modified. Incidentally, this principle is the first law of thermodynamics.

For example, the energy that once fueled Mars' hot magma core was not erased, it only radiated into space. As heat dissipated away it came to a useless state; it was no longer concentrated and organized enough to do anything. Useful energy is a physical quantity that represents the capacity to do work or cause change in a physical system, like Earth's still hot magma core showcases with every eruption.

Useful energy is what entropy slowly drains away from any organized system, from sand clocks, to volcanoes, to coffee pots.

Enter the modeling clay. You're still playing with it in your hands, and now starting to realize something.

There have been countless different forms of life, but every single one has this in common; living organisms unite matter. The atoms inside you are the same atoms that make up rocks, air, and stars. Life just brings them together and keeps them moving. Every living thing runs on metabolism: chemistry that breaks down fuel to release energy, then life uses that energy to build, repair, and copy itself. With reliable fuel, metabolism locally counteracts rising entropy by fueling cell growth.

Take a tree for example. Before a squirrel plants it and a raccoon climbs it, it needs to grow. But how?

It turns air, water, and sunlight into wood and leaves. 

A tree draws carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. Powered by sunlight, its metabolism drives a remarkable chemical process. Water molecules are split, releasing oxygen into the air, while the captured carbon is assembled into sugars, which are then converted into roots, wood, and leaves. The sun's energy becomes locked inside those chemical bonds, stored like a charged battery.

Then, when that wood burns or most commonly rots, the process runs in reverse. Fungi and microbes eat the dead but still nutritious tissue. Carbon is released and reunites with oxygen to form carbon dioxide again, the stored solar energy is released as fire or food. After a lifetime giving shelter, fruit, and seed to the forest, the tree either dissolves back into the same air, water, and soil from where it came, or becomes food for decomposers. Either way, the environment is ready for the next generation's metabolism to turn carbon, water, and sunlight into a new tree.

Life is the thing that flips the sand clock.

That's how life passes the baton to the next generation. Life organizes more matter as time goes on—if it can keep harnessing energy to stay alive.

There are no breaks. No time off. Since entropy is always increasing in nature, life must always be working to counteract it. It's a race entropy wins when it catches up. And since every location and moment in time is unique, life must customize itself to harness whatever useful energy is available regardless of time, place, and whatever has happened in the never returning past.

Hence the modeling clay in your hands.

This clay lets life mold itself to the available energy on the game board, meaning its environment. Entropy degrades the clay like everything else. Individual lifeforms get tired, weathered, and worn out. Only by birthing new clay does life stay malleable enough to find new ways to harness available energy in the changing environment and only then does life continue to grow as time goes forward.

So here is what life is: the process that turns mass into biomass by making new lifeforms. Each lifeform is a player, a bit of clay shaped to harvest energy from its own corner of the board.

Of course life is not clay. There’s something far more elegant at work.

Clay needs a hand to shape it. Life shapes itself. It began doing so somewhere between 3.7 and 4.1 billion years ago, on an Earth that looked nothing like today’s: oceans with no oxygen, volcanoes everywhere, and asteroids constantly slamming into the surface. In short, our planet looked like a disaster movie in progress.

Yet, it was here life started.

Science has some ideas how. There have been many experiments trying to spark "primordial ooze" as one theory calls the rich, chemical-laden matter of early Earth, where complex organic molecules might have first combined into self-replicating molecules. But despite trying to emulate this setting nothing's worked so far to kickstart molecules to replicate. In a world where science has stolen many of our mysteries, the emergence of life on Earth is still as wondrous as ever. And with so much uncertainty, each person is free to believe whatever they want was the start of it all.

The point is life did start.

Matter somehow began to organize, and molecules created copies of themselves. Thereafter, molecules kept finding the energy to buck entropy and keep replicating more.

With each new replication, more clay was born, and another player joined the game.

Ancient Earth had many hydrothermal vents, which scientists suspect fueled and fostered the first microbial lifeforms called anaerobic prokaryotes. These primitive organisms, similar to bacteria, represented the first sparks of life on a young, turbulent planet filled with harsh environments.

For billions of years these primitive creatures replicated, spread out, and kept going.

One group of these prokaryotes, cyanobacteria, invented the kind of photosynthesis that releases oxygen. When turning sunlight into food they produced massive amounts of atmospheric oxygen. This gradually transformed the environment in the Great Oxidation Event, and killed much of life on Earth because it was not used to so much oxygen in the atmosphere.

Although many lifeforms died, life got a massive amount of fuel. We take oxygen for granted today, but it supercharges metabolism, letting cells pull far more energy from their food. Filling the air with oxygen changed the planet for good, and eventually made room for eukaryotic cells. These cells contained a nucleus and more sophisticated structures, a significant leap in biological complexity.

Notice what happened. Oxygen was waste, and then it was fuel. Life never uses anything up, because matter and energy can’t be destroyed. Life can only put matter to use, or leave it as waste nothing living can use yet. Turn more matter into life, and the playing field grows. Leave more as waste, and it shrinks.

And the modeling clay kept growing.

Life got ever more complicated. Over eons, individual cells began cooperating in loose colonies like green algae, then eventually permanently partnered into true multi-cell organisms like primitive sponges and jellyfish. Being part of a unit let cells specialize for unique functions. Skin cells protected from the elements, muscle cells allowed locomotion, nerve cells enabled intra-organism communication.

As lifeforms kept adding cell specialization, they became more complex and developed the ability to perform more advanced functions. With more power, these organisms found new ways to postpone entropy's disordering.

The Ediacaran Period (635-541 million years ago) marked the emergence of the first complex, soft-bodied organisms. Life was no longer like simple bacteria, but becoming more like animals, featuring basic body plans and the ability to interact with their environment with cells that could detect light, the precursor to our modern eyes. These creatures, like the disc-shaped Dickinsonia, represented huge jumps in multicellular life and body complexity.

With the modeling clay self-replicating into more complex lifeforms the stage was set.

What came next was called the Cambrian Explosion, where a huge variety of creatures emerged displaying increasingly complex body structures, sensory systems, and locomotion capabilities. With an oxygen atmosphere fueling metabolic processes like never before, life could truly get creative with its modeling clay. Life could venture out into every corner of the seas to find places on the game board with energy to harvest.

The clay wasn’t only shaping itself to fit the board. It was becoming the board when it used energy to turn regular mass into biomass. Life spread over bare rock and through open water, and everywhere it went, it left the land, the seas, and the air easier to live in for whatever came next. The oxygen we breathe, and the ozone shielding us from the sun, are photosynthesis’s leftovers. The soil under every field is rock that roots and fungi ground down. Both are ready-made to spread more life.

Just think: about a tenth of the layered rock covering the continents is limestone, built from the shells and skeletons of ancient sea life. Much of our farmland, aquifers, and coral reefs are a vibrant game board built from life that supports life.

Which raises the question: if life continues over generations and ages, why don't its forms? 

Because life can either expand its range with new forms or see it dwindle with old ones. To organize ever more mass into favorable soil, air, and forms, life needed to be selective. Picking and choosing between all the options needed a mechanism. The Game of Life would need a scoreboard.

## Chapter 6—Scoreboard: What Evolution Is, The Editor of Players

You've got the Game of Life at your feet. The pieces look different to you now. The rulebook, game board, sand clock, and modeling clay are no longer gifts filled with wonder, but objects animated by the energy of purpose.

You want to play it. Are you ready? Let's press the start button!

Not so fast. Playing isn't winning. Before you venture onto your game board, let's learn what puts creatures on the scoreboard.

Just now you cough. It's small and almost goes unnoticed like a blink, breath, or other autonomic function. But alarm bells ring with this cough because it comes with a scratchy pain in your throat.

You know right away. Excitement kept you up too late last night. With so little sleep your immune system couldn't keep infection at bay. And now you're sick.

Viruses are tiny little things. Some biologists don't consider them to even be alive, still they are not much different from any other player in our understanding of the Game of Life. They are still bits of clay looking for the energy needed to stay in the game. Their strategy is simpler than most; forgo reproductive parts and instead invade another creature and hijack its cells in order to self-replicate.

Viruses are far smaller, and simpler, than the animals, plants, even bacteria they exploit. Even with this difference in size and abilities, your virus got itself on the scoreboard at your expense.

Why?

Not because it hurt you. Viruses don’t want to harm their hosts; it just happens when a host can’t replace its lost cells faster than the virus copies itself. Your virus scored because of that cough. Every cough sends its copies out to find new hosts. That’s what counts: not a short feast, but kids that carry on with a good chance of doing it all over again.

Kids are the points that put players on the scoreboard in the Game of Life.

Usually kids means direct offspring like progeny virions for viruses, saplings for trees, and children for mammals. But many species support the next generation in other ways via cooperative breeding (wolf packs, human extended families) or eusociality (ant colonies, bee hives).

Kids are valuable because of the other game pieces. The game board always changes while the game clock always ticks away. To overcome these challenges, life, the creator of players, continually needs new ones.

And new doesn’t just mean more. The recipe for every player’s clay is written in genes, made of DNA or its simpler cousin, RNA. When kids are made, genes get shuffled, and now and then one mutates, so almost every kid carries a new combination.

This is pivotal. We know every time and place in the universe is unique, that's why life shuffles its genes, grows novel forms, and travels the environment. The Game of Life is about matching the unique requirements of the environment with the unique abilities of a lifeform.

Lifeforms try to make that match through the bodies they grow, like a tree growing its branches whichever way the light is. But one genotype can only become so many phenotypes. Eventually an animal gets too thirsty, or a tree can’t find enough sunlight.

To match lifeform and environment over generations, something has to sort the players whose genes fit from those whose genes don’t.

That’s natural selection. Think of it as a coach who keeps the best-scoring players. Really, no one is choosing. Players whose kids go on to have kids of their own simply fill the next roster. Everything else a player does is how it affords them: energy saved finding water or food is energy freed up for more kids, or better-fed ones.

This strategy works very well against rising entropy. Not only has natural selection enabled life to evolve from single-cell organisms to populate the planet. It's allowed life to recover from five mass extinctions to do it all over again. Notably, no extinct species has ever come back. Life persists in whatever form the game board happens to favor.

Prioritizing offspring is how life turns a scarce energy budget into more specialized players. If life creates the many different variations in lifeforms, natural selection edits from among them to evolve new species as the board changes.

For example, brown bears are nearly identical to polar bears. They can even interbreed. By giving points to offspring, evolution slowly turned brown bears with lighter coats, living near the frigid Arctic, into polar bears. After enough points (kids), speciation happens, meaning a new bear species is made, one with a white coat, bigger paws, strong swimming, and a taste for seals. And the differences aren't only physical—speciation also shows up in behavior, like making sure to breed in line with the brief Arctic spring or knowing which seal-breathing holes to wait beside.

The great benefit of speciation is like an insurance policy. With two large bear species playing the game, if the game board gets hotter or colder, life already has a winner waiting.

The equilibrium between lifeforms, environment, and natural selection is not a truce. Every creature takes all it can when it can. Weather, competitors, distance, and time keep any one player from taking too much. Over generations, players that do more with less tend to come out ahead.

The coach doesn’t just keep players. It fills game boards.

No one intends it, but what happens as a result looks like cooperation inside an ecosystem: a community of plants and animals sharing in the work to harvest an area's resources. BBC's Planet Earth shows them well—rainforests, grasslands, coral reefs are each a web of creatures that know each other's sights and smells, often compete for the same sunlight, travel for the same grass, and yet never agreed to anything.

So here is what evolution is: new species arising as natural selection keeps the players best able to harvest their environment and reliably raise kids. But why are there so many different kinds? Why not mass-produce one winner?

Life already had one. Remember, for more than a billion years, cyanobacteria filled the seas. This simple, robust, ubiquitous creature filled the world, but one creature couldn't use all the energy on the board.

There's only so much energy, usually sunlight but sometimes volcanic heat too, available in an ecosystem. This energy budget is a major factor in an environment's "carrying capacity"; the maximum population an environment's food, water, and habitat can sustain.

A single creature can never harness all this available energy. Solar intensity, water temperatures, atmospheric chemistry, and the other game board factors change too often. And once energy is stored in living things, in leaves, in meat, even in the sore throat of a kid who stayed up too late, each becomes a meal for someone else.

So, to overcome every obstacle and harness all available energy, the coach came to favor mass-production, and diversity, to collect creatures into what we call ecosystems. Each new category helped keep the others from using too much energy or found an untapped source of energy like dead wood.

Every ecosystem has the same basic jobs. Producers, like plants and phytoplankton, use sunlight to build biomass. Plant-eaters, like caterpillars and deer, eat the producers, fertilize the soil, and sometimes spread their seeds. Predators, like lions and barracuda, keep the plant-eaters from overeating. Scavengers, like vultures and flies, eat the dead. Decomposers, like mushrooms and bacteria, break down what’s left to feed new plants. Testers, viruses and other germs, probe everyone’s defenses and thereby carry new material from one player to another. (Testers is my simplified term for this complex area.) Lastly, there are generalists like squirrels and raccoons that cross over many roles depending on what’s around.

Nobody is assigned these roles. They’re open jobs, and an open job is unclaimed energy. So something eventually evolves to fill it, not out of duty, but because an empty role is the easiest living on the board.

Remember, life doesn’t create matter. It keeps matter organized and useful. The same atoms get used again and again, in leaves, in deer, in soil. But energy is different. It enters as sunlight, passes through each player once, and leaves as heat. 

This means there's a never-ending energy flow through lifeforms.

Follow the sunlight that lands on a meadow. Grass captures only about one percent of it and locks that into leaves. A deer eats the grass and keeps about a tenth of that energy; the rest powers its walking, warms its body, and drifts off as heat. A wolf eats the deer and keeps a tenth again. Entropy takes its cut every step in the energy flow. A million units of sunlight become ten thousand in the grass, a thousand in the deer, a hundred in the wolf.

Fading energy is why the coach’s selection of better-adapted lifeforms works so well. There is so much waste that just a bit of newfound efficiency can go a long way.

Once producers lock sunlight into leaves, the game clock starts ticking. Leaves wilt, meat spoils, corpses rot, and entropy quietly carries the energy off as heat. So it’s a gold rush. Producers race to catch sunlight before it fades. Plant-eaters race to the leaves before they wither. Predators race to catch the plant-eaters before they move on. Scavengers and decomposers race to whatever’s left.

Still, what’s left is enough to fill every role and fund the next generation. Ecosystems form around available energy: a web of checks and balances, where each creature grabbing what it can happens to hold the whole in balance. Maria’s world worked the same way. Ecosystems don’t pay twice for the same job, and streaming did hers for less.

By harvesting the same limited energy over and over, the creatures of an ecosystem end up bound together—not as a team with a shared goal, but as players stuck on the same board with the same seasonal weather changes, water levels, temperature, sunlight, and geological features. Because each member must find untapped energy, they continually increase the conversion of useless matter into useful biomass, making an ecosystem an expansive force. 

That distinction matters. Remember the Jumanji movies: strangers pulled into a board game, each with a different skill—strength, speed, map-reading—who survive only by pooling those skills against the game itself. That is a real team, because they share one enemy. Life has a team like that too, and its enemy is entropy: every living thing, everywhere, is fighting the same slow pull toward cold and stillness. Against *that*, all living things harvest energy just to hold themselves together. Not figuratively. Stop, and the atoms go their separate ways.

But against each other, the players are rivals. A polar bear and a seal aren't teammates—they want opposite things. They are **boardmates**: creatures whose games are tangled because they're playing the same board. The seal and the bear. The virus and your cough. The DVD and the streaming box. Individually, they have no shared purpose but when they share a game board they make a sustainable ecosystem.

Put simply: every lifeform is doing as little as it can while still getting the job done. Not from laziness of character, but because energy is scarce and whatever is saved goes to growth, defense, or offspring. And when organisms get more out of the same budget, the ecosystem supports more life on the same sunlight.

Evolution keeps finding players that do more with less: aerodynamic birds, streamlined fish, hopping kangaroos, shaggy bison. And boardmates, while always competing at the edges, also reciprocate, on purpose or not. None can remove the others entirely, because no lifeform can run an ecosystem alone.

When every role is just barely paying its own way, the board is in equilibrium.

A flower feeding a bee is an obvious example of boardmates reciprocating, but for a non-obvious example, take your virus. It wants exactly what you want: another turn. In civilization we support the sick with extra food, rest, and medicine. But in the wild, germs press hardest on bodies already running low. A deer too underfed to fight off an autumn infection was unlikely to survive winter, and its share of food goes to the herd. Germs also hit whatever is most common, keeping populations mixed. Nobody arranged it, but testers keep the herd within its budget and varied enough for the next change.

The polar bear is the same truth from the other side. She takes every seal she can catch—there's no restraint in her. But she *can't* take them all, and that isn't luck. She and the seal have shaped each other for hundreds of thousands of years, her speed against its escape, until neither can win outright. Neither limit is a choice; they're an adaptation of bear and seal being near equilibrium. The proof is the penguin—drop that same bear on an Antarctic shore and she kills until nothing's left, because nothing there was ever shaped by her. Even though they are playing the same game against entropy, a seal is a boardmate, a penguin is not. 

Every lifeform is built to do more with less. But a lifeform cannot tell the difference between finding a cheaper way to pay the bill and telling yourself the bill isn't owed. A polar bear keeps seals from overfishing and contributes to the efficient energy flow of an ecosystem. The easy kill of the penguin feels like the same instinct. It isn't, she's skipping out on the bill and the boardmates can only replace her excess for so long.

Lifeforms battle at the edges for daily survival, whether it's hunter chasing prey, or trees racing for sunlight. They need to battle, because over time, this keeps each other doing their ecological role. Wanting to kill each other but never eliminate each other is not a contradiction. Schoolmates and bandmates fight for spots all the time. That doesn't mean they don't have a shared interest too.

Same for our boardmates and the calories they must find in their shared ecosystem to push entropy back for another day.

The last lesson of this gift is in its name: it's the Game of Life, not the Game of You, Me, or anyone else. We don't win by combating a team of rivals—we win by making our own moves on a board we share, while all of us, together and unknowing, hold back the dark a little longer.

Outsiders aren't evil of course. They are simply those not integrated into the board. The blights that took the American chestnut, the beetle killing the ash, the disease that hollowed out the orange groves are adapted to other ecosystems. A polar bear loose in Antarctica would be one more of them: no penguin ever learned to fear her.

Of course, individual creatures know none of this. They are bits of clay already hardened, inside the game, unable to stand above it. So each one simply fights to survive—bison bulls battling for a herd, a tiger running down a deer, a Venus flytrap folding over a wasp.

But that's the game seen too close—just a small part of it.

The whole game is about scoring points: offspring who stand a good chance of having offspring of their own. Kids are literally the future, which is why the Game of Life makes the perfect Christmas or birthday present.

Now that you know how the rulebook, game board, sand clock, modeling clay, and scoreboard work together—let's play.

Not this game, though. Its sequel, which runs by rules so much like these. Let's play the Game of Technological Life. Let's play techvolution.

## Story—The Bell

Most people assume insurance adjusters succeed by underpaying claims. Lowball, short-change, screw people over—that's what the public thinks.

In car insurance claims, at least, we routinely overpaid. Not out of generosity, but desperation.

Here's why: we were barely measured on payment accuracy or customer satisfaction. But we were exactly measured on inbound caller hold time. Head office promised insurance brokers that customers wouldn't wait longer than 30 seconds—it makes for a great selling point. Brokers make commission on sales, not claims, so they just wanted their customers to be served promptly when making a claim. Importantly, we always knew (and were reminded) that an unhappy broker could send customers to other companies.

So the inbound caller hold time became everything. 

To monitor it, the call-in queue was displayed on monitors for everyone to see. To police it, the company played a bell in our headsets. When someone was on hold, it rang: DING, DING, DING. A monotone alarm that never stopped until you picked up. A sound fit for a memory test or torture chamber.

When the bell rang too long, the public screen would go red. Then a manager would direct message: "ANSWER THE QUEUE." If that didn't work, they'd force calls through to us without warning—sometimes hanging up on whoever you were already helping.

We had a lot of other work to do. Emails from waiting customers, calling police departments for investigations, making outbound calls to repair or rental stores. But none of that was tracked nearly as much. Just the bell.

Under that much time pressure, we adapted. Some worked on weekends. Others were very curt to customers. Some adjusters took the customer's word and didn't investigate fault. But the easiest time-saver was, as our managers would often say when money was the dispute, "Just pay it".

Anything to stop the bell.

Adjusters' (and managers') personalities adapted too. Many became irritable, even withdrawn (usually those who tried to follow the rules exactly). Paramedics even visited a few times to cart coworkers away. I had to massage my jaw on the drive home from stuttering through so many rushed conversations.

We were often more stressed than the people who'd just been in crashes.

All because someone in head office—who'd never done our job—made a promise about hold times and never looked further than that one metric.

The company got what they measured: fast answer rates.

They also got inflated claims, burned-out adjusters, higher insurance rates, and a system that rewarded speed over truth.

One more claim down. One less caller to make that freaking bell ring.

## Chapter 7—What Does the Game of Life Teach Us?

Before we press start on the Game of Technological Life, there's something to ask again.

Will AI take your job?

First, do we even know what our job is? We know the labels: tool man, distribution manager, executive, employee.

But natural selection can’t speak English and will never try to. Accolades can't impress, pleas go unheard, firings are ignored. The coach only sees what keeps our atoms together and our kids coming: energy, harvested somewhere along the energy flow of food, shelter, and other adaptations, before entropy carries it off. For you, me, wolves, squirrels, and raccoons, that’s the job underneath every label, from producer to decomposser, hunter-gatherer and explorer to farmer, teacher, and king.

That’s what happened to Maria. DVDs by mail and streaming shared the board for years. D video dics of course used digital technology, as did her company logistics system, customer website, and more. And when disc content went online, streaming users found a shorter route to a movie tonight, with no drive, no late fee, no store, and no distribution center. Something was lost: the video store was a cool gathering place. But far more work was saved. And because energy is limited the Game of Life scores points for whichever new form finds untapped energy, whether in genes, behavior, or tools.

In hindsight, the winning adaptation is often obvious. The hard part is seeing it ahead of time, and embracing it, instead of slapping a label on the change and calling it wrong to keep our own self-identity alive.

But labels can’t stop the game. There are a million names for the rulebook’s laws, the game board’s changing environment, the sand clock’s rising disorder, the modeling clay’s many forms, and the coach’s natural selection. We can use labels to help us, like any other adaptation, but none of them change the rules.

So to answer whether AI will take your job, we have to look past the labels to the effectual truth: which tools your job runs on, and where it sits in the energy flow of our technological ecosystem.

Before we can answer it, there’s something to say about the Game of Life itself.

The Game of Life is a fairly accurate way to describe life on Earth, but it’s a metaphor, and specialists could pick at it. Entropy is more complex than “things fall apart,” and the everyday versions of nature’s laws break down at extremes, near black holes or close to light speed, where physicists need relativity and quantum mechanics to predict outcomes. But those extremes rarely touch our lives, and none of it changes entropy’s overwhelming pull: order breaks down, energy spreads out. For everyday life, the rulebook to the Game of Life holds true.

What deserves more detail is how new players get chosen. Natural selection isn’t the only force at work.

There’s sexual selection, where mates choose partners for traits like a peacock’s tail or a stag’s antlers. There’s genetic drift, where genes spread by pure chance, like a rare disease becoming common in an isolated village because a few founders carried it. There’s gene flow, where separated populations meet again and mix, as polar and brown bears are doing now. And there’s co-evolution, where two species become each other’s environment, like the flowers and bees we met as boardmates, each pushing the other for millions of years.

Then there’s epigenetics. If your DNA is a library, epigenetics is the librarian deciding which books get read. The genes you inherit don’t change during your life, but which ones are switched on can, sometimes before you’re born. Dutch children who were in the womb during the 1944–45 famine grew up with higher rates of obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. And since a girl’s eggs form while she’s still a fetus, that one famine touched three generations at once. Whether such effects last longer is still debated for mammals; in plants and some worms, they clearly do.

But there’s a hard limit. Biologists call it the Weismann barrier: what your body learns doesn’t get written back into the genes you pass on. A lifetime of skill and hardship dies with you. A few biologists question how absolute that is. It looks unlikely, but it’s a healthy reminder to keep our laws open to question.

These other forces add new players but over the long run, every player still has to harvest enough to raise kids who raise kids and so on. That's why natural selection remains the coach in our game.

And that hard limit is where our story turns. The Weismann barrier says biology can’t pass on what we learn. But tools can. To understand the Game of Technological Life, we just need to see how similar it is to the biological version.

For starters, we have a historical legacy of using animal products as technological adaptations. A few examples are fur coats, water skins, beasts of burden, carrier pigeons, and hunting dogs, but there are countless others. Harvesting our tools from nature means assessing the strengths and weaknesses of animals and thus being a sort of coach, selecting species to solve specific problems. 

Of course we developed our own tools. From the spears of the Stone Age, to the chisels of the Bronze Age, and all the countless tools since, making and using a specific set of tools is often what defines a person, culture, society, and civilization. Many of our personal names, from Smith (metal working), Cooper (barrel making), Weaver (cloth weaving), and so many others, show how interwoven tools are with human identities. 

Biology and technology have so much crossover we often design our technologies while looking over the shoulder of nature.

Today, there's even a field called biomimicry; the emulation of natural models, systems, and elements for the purpose of inventing new technologies. Among its many creations is Velcro, copied from the fuzzy seed burrs that stick to our clothes. Many airplanes, drones, robotics, and other technological designs get their inspiration from the biological products of natural selection.

Biology and technology cross over so well because they face the same problems. Both need structure to fight gravity: skeletons and framing. Both need to move efficiently through air and water: birds and planes, fish and ships. Both need to manage heat: fur and furnaces, panting and air conditioning. We even chart both with the same kind of family tree. Same rulebook, same game board, same sand clock, so the same pressures shape both.

We know how biology responds to these challenges, and when was the last time you, or anyone, grew a winter coat of hair to combat Minnesota winters? Do parents ever tell a kid to increase skull bone strength before climbing a mountain? Does anyone create more fat to store food before a long road trip?

Of course not.

We see evolution in the natural world but not in our own lives. Watch a documentary about a struggling polar bear and nobody argues; the ice went, the polar bear is in trouble. Say the same about Maria—her environment changed and her adaptation stopped working well inside it—and it sounds like we're making an excuse or assigning blame. We hope the same pressure of an environment changing somehow won't apply to us, granting ourselves exemptions no other animal gets.

Look to today and you'll see the same thing. So many know so little about their tools' place in the ecosystem they can't say if they'll have a job next month. We have one vocabulary for animals based on dealing with scientific facts and another for ourselves, where we describe the same thing using personal desires or ideological stories.

We know the point of contact between entropy and biology is a set of genes—variants that either harvest enough energy to make it into the next generation, or don't. The point of contact between entropy and us is almost always a tool, yet we do not think evolution pressures us as well.

Even with all these indications that we're using technology to survive the Game of Life, few people label themselves as their tool. Nationality, religion, ideology, job title, even sports teams and comic books take up more room in our identities than the tools we use to stay alive.

That's why this book wants you to embrace being a technological being. Not because it's an interesting idea, but because it makes us realize we are playing the Game of Life, and technology is our primary means of postponing entropy—the everyday work of staying organized against dispersal which we need to stay alive long enough to pass on the torch.

We see this truth in the natural world. We should be able to see it in our technological one too.

The rulebook, game board of environments, sand clock of time, bits of clay, and a scoreboard are all there. As is the circle of producers, plant-eating consumers, predators, scavengers, decomposers, and testers in our own ecosystems too. This is happening all around us.

So why don't we see it?

Because our coach is unconscious of what it's selecting for. Picking the boardmates without realizing you're picking often clouds the view. Here is a quick story to explain.

There's this crab, Heikegani, that's native to Japan. It's rather small with long legs. It would be another unknown crab except it has an interesting feature. Its shell looks like a human face. In particular, an angry samurai.

Why?

The crab's name gives a clue. Their namesake is the Taira clan who dominated medieval Japan and were commonly known as the Heike. Their warriors fought a massive civil war against the Minamoto clan from 1180–1185.

The war was decided at the massive Dan-no-ura naval battle in western Japan.

The Heike lost and Minamoto's leader Yoritomo became the first shōgun to rule over Japan. This was a cultural and political turning point in Japanese history. After it, for seven centuries, Japan was ruled not by emperors, but by samurai warriors and shōguns. Local legend said Heikegani crabs are reincarnations of the Heike warriors defeated at the battle. The battle is told in _The Tale of the Heike_, a chronicle of ancient Japanese history.

That's one interpretation for the crab's distinctive feature.

Carl Sagan, the noted American astronomer and educator, popularized a different interpretation. In his book and documentary _Cosmos: A Personal Voyage_ Sagan says the Heikegani crabs were caught by local fishermen who occasionally saw faces in the shell. Out of respect for ancient fallen soldiers, the fishermen returned them to the sea, while harvesting the unlucky crabs who didn't carry the spirits of an ancestor. As the years went by more newly hatched crabs had the genes that made their shell look like a face. Eventually, all the Heikegani crabs had what looked like a human face on their shell.

Human beings had selected winning crabs, but for unnatural reasons, which is why the Heike crabs are a classic example of "artificial selection"; humans choosing which individuals of a species reproduce, based on traits we find desirable and thereby accelerating evolution in a direction nature didn't select for on its own.

We've of course done this when turning wild wheat into a crop, wolves into dogs, horses into riding animals, and so on.

There is another version of the Heike crab story. There's actually no record of Japanese fishermen ever selecting crabs for human faces. The crab isn't even a staple of any known diets. Evolutionary biologist Joel Martin thinks the crab's distinctive muscles and shell are simply built the way they are because natural selection says it works. Humans only see a face because of human pareidolia, our tendency to impose a meaningful interpretation on patterns, so that we easily detect meaning in places where there is none.

So we're doing today exactly what we imagine the fishermen did: seeing a face in a crab shell.

We often do the same in the stars or clouds in the sky, where we see horses, chariots, and endless other objects inside floating water vapor, or balls of gas unimaginably far from each other.

These visions only make sense from our own vantage point. Meaning while playing on the emergent game board and clock in the Game of Technological Life, we are often chasing the shadows of our own psyches.

So, the Heikegani crab showcases something very important for our winning the game. We often replace nature's coach with ourselves and use artificial selection to pick our boardmates.

Whether or not the fishermen ever did it, the story holds both lessons. Either we were selecting without realizing we were the selectors, or we were seeing a face that was never there. Both are what happens when we don’t examine what we’re selecting for.

As any medical doctor or forestry manager knows, a self-aware selector picks on the real pressures the environment imposes—which, over time, is what natural selection does in any case. Self-aware artificial selection aims at the same target blind nature does, but it's reached on purpose instead of by countless deaths. It's how tool-using species find their way back to nature; all to "rack up more wins" in the Game of Life.

Artificial selection can point at the true target or a false one. Self-awareness is what turns it toward the true kind.

Evolution's seminal book is, of course, Charles Darwin's _On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection_. Most people know the first part, but skip over the second part "by means of natural selection." As the Heikegani crab story highlights we often artificially play the game. This book is about making our selections more responsive to the real problems of our game board—the actual pressures our environment imposes like road congestion, broken furnances, car crashes.

Fittingly, Darwin’s book doesn’t open with nature. Its first chapter is about pigeon fanciers and livestock breeders: people choosing which animals breed. Darwin started with the selection we do on purpose to explain the selection nature does blindly. This book extends that selection to our tools, which also need new versions and a steady supply of energy to keep entropy from tearing them apart.

And so, to win the Game of Technological Life, we do not need to invent anything from scratch. We already study nature, copy its designs, and build tools to harvest energy.

We already play the game!

To earn a much better score, we only need to get out of our heads. Meaning: as individuals, we stop selecting on the face in the shell, and start selecting on what the board actually rewards.

Happily, we've done this in many other fields: physics, chemistry, geology, and medicine. The history of these journeys teaches us that we can improve upon our mind's creation by assessing the state of nature as it truly is and thereby move from mythological, to philosophical, and finally to scientific levels of understanding. However, history also tells us this is a dangerous voyage filled with winding roads, dead ends, and other shadowy places artificial knowledge takes its passengers.

We're pressing start anyway.

Buckle up, the first challenge is not easy. We must learn to defend ourselves against going down the wrong path of artificial human identities by understanding what human nature truly is. Let's meet the best you, the natural player of the game.

## Chapter 8—Our Base Player Nature

Nature has many meanings: the oceans and mountains of the natural world, the “forces of nature” that run the universe, Mother Nature’s power to create life, and the old question “what is its nature?”, which asks what something truly is.

The natural player of the game is the one who accepts all of these: the board keeps changing, the rules don’t bend, and life keeps making new forms. To meet that player, we start where our genes did.

We can chart ourselves with greater context. The tree of life describes the relationships between organisms both living and extinct. It's a huge list of producers, plant-eating consumers, predators, scavengers, decomposers, and testers, which continually die off and update to keep up with the changing game board.

Humanity is on this tree like any other being. Our last common ancestor with our hominid cousins chimpanzees and bonobos lived between perhaps eight to six million years ago. Long in our timeframe, but very minimal in the evolutionary one.

To put that in perspective, we share more DNA with our ape relatives than our pet cats share with tigers; and any cat owner will tell you the physiological, sensory, and predatory, similarities between the small and large felines. The point to remember is that any investigation of human nature must root itself into our being part of the animal kingdom. 

We need to start here because our genes were selected like anyone else's. Living under the stars and up against the whims of nature, our DNA passed the entire gauntlet of trials—from cold and heat to hunger, aging, and limited resources—and was specifically selected to play in the Game of Life.

Modern humans still have all the perseverance, edge, grit, and other abilities and limitations from our ancestors. This manifests in many ways.

Some are physiological. Such as our offspring have prolonged vulnerability after birth. To provide extended care their parents typically mate for life. Our chimpanzee and bonobo relatives mate year round because lush jungles have no need to limit birth times. We kept that practice after leaving the jungles, perhaps because relatively high infant mortality, estimated as high as 40% for some Bronze Age peoples, means couples must be ready to replace lost members faster.

Something else humans kept from our animal kingdom heritage, is that we do not naturally get along. While human genes had much in common, we still formed tribes to protect our specific bits of clay. Early humans lived in communities from a dozen up to 200 members. Persistent natural selection pressure meant the ever-present chance of thirst, hunger, and disease. This pressure could get so high that in some ancient societies, warfare, likely over resources, may have caused as much as 30–40% of all deaths. Such was the drive to be picked by the coach.

We did of course evolve away from our hominid ancestors and begin to play the game with technological tools, and that required drastic changes to our biology.

The most notable was trading easy tree climbing for amazing endurance running. Body hair overheated us during our long runs, so it got replaced by bare skin covered with sweat glands. Our vocal tract developed to make increasingly complex sounds. And, perhaps the most important change, our thumbs became fully opposable, capable of both precise and strong grip.

Each of these biological adaptations got more pronounced over time because they kept earning points on the procreation scoreboard. Meaning that, like a hummingbird and flower growing their beaks and petals to match each other's uniqueness, with each generation our bodies were forming around the requirements of technology in a case of co-evolution.

Using fossils and DNA, we can track our biological evolution.

The earliest fossils in our own genus, Homo (Latin for human), are about 2.8 million years old, from Ethiopia. Thereafter, we evolved into several known groups, including Homo erectus, Denisovans, Neanderthals, and us, Homo sapiens, and the list grows as more fossil and DNA evidence is discovered.

With distance running, sweat, speech, and thumbs, humans got many points in the Game of Life. Every new generation could better hold spears and organize ourselves to reliably chase, exhaust, kill, and defend protein rich food no ape ever could. We learned to crave sugar for the rare energy boosts it gave, but being omnivorous, could gather something to eat year round; especially because we learned to use rocks and sticks to dig up, open, or grind an increasingly large menu of calorie dense roots, nuts, and seeds.

All these adaptations opened up new opportunities on the game board. Every new food source, water hole, cave home was a supply chain worthy of passing on. A great job for our growing speech abilities. Crucially, this included the value of charred meat, likely learned from scavenging food after brush fires. Perhaps as long as 1.8 million, but at least 1 million years ago, humans started our own fires to cook, and therefore predigest, our food.

Fire of course was pivotal to technology. Like how plants use sunlight to rearrange matter into life, fire rearranges matter into ceramics, bronze, and iron. But, the two are linked. The heat of the sun is nuclear powered. Volcanoes are radioactive decay. Fire is chemical, and it needs two things only life provides: oxygen in the air, released by cyanobacteria and plants, and fuel, the stored sunlight in wood, coal, and gas. Even much of our iron ore formed when that early oxygen rusted the oceans, and cement is made from ancient shells. Technology is the offspring of biology.

As tools and fire made digestion easier, requiring about 10-30% less energy per calorie, our jaws could get smaller. Our brains had both the space and energy to grow. The human skull grew from around 500 cubic centimeters (cc) of Australopithecus to around 1,350 cc of early Homo sapiens. Although brain size does not directly correlate with intelligence, it generally trends that way. Interestingly, we kept the hair on our heads to help shield, cool, and heat the organ using up energy to fuel billions of neurons.

So, while early humans' co-evolution with technology made us increasingly unique in nature, we still kept much of our genes and instincts unchanged. These two influences together make up the genetics that, 300,000 years ago, would form our Homo sapiens branch of life.

Our species’ genes never stopped changing. A large 2026 ancient-DNA study led by Ali Akbari and David Reich found that natural selection actually sped up after farming, peaking in the Bronze Age, when new technologies upended how people lived. Selection on genes linked today to cognitive performance was strongest between about 4,000 and 2,000 years ago. Then, over the last 2,000 years, it all but disappeared. The field is young and developing fast. My reading is that selection didn’t stop; it shifted from weighing our bodies to weighing our tools, just as steel, sails, and scrolls took over building, moving, and remembering.

This is our basic genetic history, and we need to talk about it for several reasons.

There are about 3.2 billion base pairs of human DNA. As Siddhartha Mukherjee points out in his classic _The Gene: An Intimate History_, that means there are more variations of human beings possible than there are atoms in the universe. How wonderful! We will never run out of unique people to play the game. Especially because genetic drift, epigenetics, and other forms of selection can make endless amazing, and sometimes unfortunate, variations of humans.

Yet, and here's the point, you will never meet a person who isn't made up of our genetic heritage and all the capabilities and limitations that brings.

Like sloths or elephants, we carry instincts common to nearly everyone. Same board, same trials, so the coach shaped us all toward the same answers. It's why most people need eight hours of sleep, run on about 2,000 calories a day, can keep up only about 150 meaningful relationships, crave sugar, dread public speaking, and stay prone to tribal loyalty even while capable of real sacrifice. Stone Age, Iron Age, or today, it's the same creature.

Our genes don’t fix our future; the belief that they do is called genetic determinism. We can aspire to be different; break molds, boundaries, and records. Years of speech therapy and practice certainly makes me a proponent of improving upon our nature, but still, never can we succeed by disregarding it.

Our creative minds do not automatically know that, but as the Weismann barrier says, while genetic expression can change and help offspring somewhat adapt to the environment, every person is still stuck with the unchangeable genomes regardless of our ideas, beliefs, or life experiences.

Any identity that ignores our past is artificial, and leads to a dead end.

We've already met two such attempts, the communist and fascist ideologies that scarred human history so badly. Among their many errors, one proclaimed humans naturally selfless unless tainted with a corrupted class history, condemning untold millions to mass purges. Ant specialist E.O. Wilson summed it up: "Wonderful theory, wrong species." Selfless obedience is how ants behave, not hominids.

The other said "blood" should inspire ancient tribal loyalties throughout millions of strangers. Cohesion required "purity" and therefore justified dooming all outsiders and told long-time friends, colleagues, and neighbors to learn to hate someone. This is how small-band tribal humans lived, not humans whose technological ecosystem connects them with millions of strangers daily. It was also a belief that mistook the blueprint for the building. Every lifeform is changed and molded by its upbringing and environment and every lifeform needs good boardmates, whatever their blood.

Any identity is artificial when it expects people to behave in ways we never did when evolving in the Game of Life. A natural lifeform is one that locally postpones entropy—organizing itself and its surroundings into structures that can pass the torch forward.

Of course, we are not solely defined by our animal kingdom past. But human genes cannot be wished away with human words. Happily, while our nature defines our basic needs and limits, something else motivates our souls. Touching it is how we best tell the difference between artificial and natural; one requires coercion while the other is so good it makes people, especially kids, smile when opening a box and seeing it for the first time.

## Chapter 9—Spreading the Technological Soil

Why are humans so similar across the world, despite living in completely different environments? The answer lies in our shared origin in Africa and the technological co-evolution that followed us out.

The natural human player of the game is rooted in shared biological instincts and requirements. People need to eat, love, have shelter, hope, and many other things. Among our limitations is that we are social like mammals but not eusocial like ants: will never embrace working for others or give blind loyalty without getting some benefit in return. Regardless of the many artificial attempts to impose this identity on humans, these are pretty universal features of mankind across time and geography.

There is a reason why using pure genotype or phenotype perspectives to explain and solve humanity's problems both fail. Both genetic and cultural evolution must operate together to build the extended phenotype. Technololgy is why Homo sapiens needed and have both very similar physiology and very similar culture.

Using our bodies and minds to create new technology is what motivates our souls. It puts smiles on our faces so easily we give them as gifts to kids who can't speak. No one has to force a kid to play with a new tool, we naturally take joy in touching, learning, and using this amazingly new creations of life.

That's why no human population is biologically locked out of learning any tool. With open learning, any human can learn to use any tool.

This shared drive to create new technology starts off with our shared history. Neanderthals were mostly in Europe, Denisovans in East and South East Asia, Homo sapiens evolved in the huge landmass below the Saharan desert, somewhere in East Africa. The place was full of water and wildlife and so capable of feeding early humans year round. With a healthy carrying capacity, lots of tribes could live, and the huge geography made it harder for migration interactions to blend groups.

Geographers call this the friction of distance. It's important because relative tribe isolation allowed many adaptations a chance to offer themselves to the coach's selection. The legacy of this time period lives on today; there is still more genetic diversity in sub-Saharan Africa than in the rest of the entire world.

In this hotbed of evolution, Homo sapiens became a nomadic hunter-gatherer group, with people and lifestyle that we would recognize today. We're so close to these persons that, because of a genetic quirk of mitochondrial DNA, we can even trace all modern humans to a single common woman ancestor who lived approximately 155,000 years ago, colloquially called "Mitochondrial Eve". 

It's perhaps comical to imagine, but a modern human could be transported back to our ancestors in East Africa and—eventually—do fine. We could physically perform the way of life. Community driven hunting, resource gathering, and child rearing in groups where we knew everyone's name, personality, and goals. Tribes had hunting and foraging grounds across savannas and woodlands. While looking for our next meal, the average daily trip was about 6-9 miles (10-15 kilometers) a day, probably not a coincidence that is about the caloric expenditure many people expend today while staying in shape.

Many Homo sapiens tribes lived in Africa for thousands of generations. But our biological adaptations had maxed out. Extreme distance walking and running could get us calorie dense food and forage, but with our hungry brains, vulnerable kids, little fat or hair to keep us warm, we were at a biological endpoint as a species.

Anything we need to outsmart the emergent game board and go faster than the sand clock was very much noticed.

So, the coach kept picking Homo sapiens who developed better tools, crafted warmer clothes, and passed on more knowledge into complex social structures connected with evermore gossip and teachings. Around 100,000-70,000 years ago, East Africa was such a hotbed of innovation it's called the Cognitive Revolution. Some say the development was more gradual, but in any event Homo sapiens developed symbolic thinking, complex language, advanced tool-making, and artistic expression. This change allowed humans to create art, plan for the future, develop spiritual beliefs, and cooperate at larger scales than ever before.

A spear outlives its maker, can be employed by a user, and inspires a tinkerer to make a change. As does a fur coat, a bone needle, a stone blade. What our genes couldn’t pass on, our tools and teachings could.

It's not hard to imagine our ancestors—with mostly modern bodies and brains—sitting around campfires after a day's work. With wonderfully perceptive eyes and budding storytelling, they fashioned characters and events to explain the Game of Life they sensed they were playing. Just looking up at the Milky Way must have been the most awesome experience. With a virtual blank slate, persons imagined (or realized depending on the reader's beliefs) that beings, titans, and gods held the strings on the people playing at life down on Earth.

Before the Cognitive Revolution we were physically modern humans, after it we behaved like modern humans. A modern person would be relatively happy here. Ecologist Gordon H. Orians research suggests that our current love of a house on a lake, with the mountains behind us and a grassland in front, is from our evolving in East Africa during this time period.

Yet, the relative safety, abundant resources, and pleasant weather were soon to be a happy memory.

Our adaptable hands, feet, cognition, and tools, allowed early modern humans to navigate most any environmental challenge on the African game board. That also meant, after so many millions of years and various forms of humanity, nature finally created a human ready to venture across the world.

Or perhaps it's better to say, technology had evolved the perfect match to seed itself worldwide. And the bits of clay that ventured out could find much scoreboard success. And out we went!

There were waves of Out of Africa Homo sapiens migrations. We got to Eurasia 125,000-100,000 years ago, Australia around 65,000 years ago, the Americas about 15,000 years ago, and remote islands such as Hawaii, Easter Island, Madagascar, and New Zealand between the years 300 to 1280. At each place, Homo sapiens gradually mixed with, absorbed, or outlasted local populations of other human species.

The timing is still debated among scientists. Unfortunately there was no written history back then, so piecing together events will always be difficult. But the Cognitive Revolution and Out of Africa migrations were probably mutually reinforcing processes. Better cognition enabled migration, which created new selective pressures and opportunities that further refined human culture and tools. Rather than one causing the other, they likely fed into each other—a positive feedback loop where cognitive advances enabled exploration, and exploration across new environments drove further innovations.

However it happened, by about 40,000 years ago, archaic humans were effectively gone. But for trace amounts of old DNA (about 1-5%), all humans from then on were Homo sapiens with endless DNA varieties possible but still with relatively consistent nature, physiology, and culture. While our bits of clay were destined to be continually remixed, they would never be so fundamentally different that language, beliefs, or tools had limited spread. 

By mixing genotype and phenotype variation, humans were doing what life always does—producing evolved variations—only now with their extended phenotype, technology, as the medium. The tool itself was thereafter being selected by the coach as Homo sapiens kept adapting our cultures and co-evolving tools to nearly every game board on Earth. From north to south, desert to jungle, chilling cold to sweating heat, our adaptability let us outpace the sand clock and keep scoring procreation points around the world.

Now the extended phenotype—technology—had the uniformity it needed in humans to sprout across the globe. Just as early plants ground rock into soil and slowly allow for more life, a uniform humanity became the soil technology could take root in anywhere. Once it got hold, its ecosystem spread far and wide, with humans often deciding which tools lived and which died.

## Chapter 10—Planting, Building, and Playing the New Game

Humanity has a base identity using both our genes and upbringing, or genotype and phenotype in nature's eyes. Homo sapiens have a shared culture and physiology that when put together helped us create, use, and spread technology around the world.

As we spread across the world, we created extended phenotypes according to the problems of whatever emergent game board we encountered. Wooden spear tips became flint rock or obsidian, caves mammoth tusk tents, fur skins were sewn into clothing. But, hunting and gathering with tools was still mostly as before.

The invention of agriculture was the dawn of civilization. It's called the Neolithic Revolution. It occurred independently at different times. First taking root in Southwest Asia's Fertile Crescent around 10,000 BC, China 9,000 BC, Mesoamerica 5,000 BC and other regions afterward. As Jared Diamond notes in his landmark _Guns, Germs, and Steel_, each civilization was heavily influenced by the local wildlife and resources, however most locations started some mixture of permanent human settlements, the domestication of animals, clay pottery, and in some places later on, metal making.

In every instance civilization drastically increased the productivity of land. Farming fed far more people per acre, even though early farmers often worked longer and ate worse than foragers, eventually humans learned to master farm water irrigation, crop rotation, and had a relatively stable food supply throughout the year.

Freed from the daily hunting and gathering treks, humans had more time on our hands and crucially more jobs to do. We strayed more from the wilderness realm and built new things, divided the labor to do it, and created political regimes to keep everyone organized and working. Within a few thousand years the Stone Age turned into the Copper, Bronze, then Iron Age, each stage remade civilization's farming, logistics, housing and other staples based on an amazing new material available to build the powerful new tools.

As civilization progressed with each passing year, our nature was increasingly technological. Not necessarily because that's what human nature wanted, or because that's what made us happier, but for the same reason all species such as polar bears and Darwin's finches find success. Because the paler bear and the more unique beak is better adapted to the game board and so gets more resources and therefore births more points on the scoreboard. The coach always selects whoever can—temporarily, of course—outrun the sand clock, which otherwise wears down what life builds into useless matter.

With each new technological selection, the coach was carving a new type of player out of life's living clay.

Think how weird and wonderful it is that all humans can breed with each other, live anywhere, and use any tool. Inventing and modifying takes much study, but regardless of the complexity most anyone can use any tool. For a modern example, most every tech nerd has been asked to help someone set up a new iPhone or printer. Yet, once set up, the owners of these tools can easily connect with friends, stream movies, process insurance claims.

This isn't an accident. There's no way for a person to invent a hoe or sword that a stranger or enemy can't use. Tools spread to where the problems existed. That could be water wells giving fresh hydration, to stone walls giving protection against weather and foe, and when online streaming replaced the commute to get and return DVDs.

Even today, it's not hard to imagine how a person living in a jungle could use an iPad and satellite internet to better his life, if only to see where the loggers are headed to next.

Our real-life pains are the thing technology will never feel, yet we designed technology to solve them in a classic case of co-evolution.

Turning us into its users, empowering us to live anywhere on the globe, technology created the soil in which it grows. Human pains, pleasures, and passions guide technology where to evolve next.

Co-evolving with technology had a profound effect on our genotypes and phenotypes. It’s why so much of our individual variety, which harsher environments might have trimmed away, found a place. Civilization allowed more human variations to live, by liberating it from constraints of the African plain environment.

In return, humanity had to seed, inhabit, work, and harvest from a different ecosystem.

Physically, we vary enormously—in muscle mass, fat distribution, how efficiently we use oxygen. None of this is one gene flipping on or off. Each trait is polygenic: hundreds of tiny genetic variants, each nudging the outcome a little, adding up to a wide spectrum rather than a few fixed types. That's why, as humanity met new problems, our variety meant someone was usually better suited to provide an edge to get the job done, whether sprinting fast, swimming against the current, or hammering steel.
 
And it's not just our bodies that give abundant variations. Socially we have a wide spectrum of people too. The introvert drained by a crowd, the extrovert fed by it, and from loving reliable tradition to needing to explore risky new things. Mentally there are people of endless different abilities too. The person who recites histories from memory, another doing math in her head, a third who plays music no one taught her. No two humans have builds, temperaments, or minds that are quite alike.

As civilization advanced, more of the options in our 3.2 billion DNA base pairs were coming out that might have been trimmed away in the biological world, but were finding niches in a home in civilization and therefore the Game of Technological Life.

I often wonder how my speech impediment would have changed in earlier times. At least in nomadic times, I think it might have been an advantage. Tracking game, avoiding predators, evading competitors, it might help to be naturally quiet. But as society grew, I likely would have missed out on many opportunities to advocate for myself in political conversations at best or tossed aside at worst, explained within the stories of the time as "cursed" or a simpleton of some kind.

Of course I don't know this, but I only mean to say our different personalities, body types, mental abilities, and other characteristics often find a good home in civilization. For myself, not being able to talk too much, undoubtedly changed personal development. It made it easier to empathize with others while also making me indignant against social rules about what is polite or acceptable to say because these add another layer of complexity when I was often happy to say anything at all.

As noted geneticist Theodosius Dobzhansky said, "The pervasiveness of genetic variation provides the biological foundation of human individuality." The point for us being, the technological ecosystem had many open spots, and often our wonderfully unique personalities fit into these niches.

Claudius, Epictetus, and Franklin D. Roosevelt each lived with a physical disability. Claudius had a limp, a tremor, and a speech impediment, and his own family tried to keep him out of public view. Epictetus was born a slave and, by tradition, was lame. Roosevelt was paralyzed from the waist down by polio and used a wheelchair and crutches. In a harsher world, their bodies might have counted against them. In civilization, Claudius became a Roman emperor, Epictetus became a celebrated philosopher, and Roosevelt was America's president four times.

History has endless examples of such people. Let's take Isaac Newton and Steve Jobs. On the biological scoreboard these men were ordinary or oddities—Newton likely died a virgin, Jobs had a family but washed his feet in toilets and was often very hard to work with. On the technological one they scored higher than almost anyone who ever lived: Newton’s laws still guide every bridge and rocket, and Jobs’s iPhones number in the billions.

The list continues. One must wonder about the mindset of our many political dissidents. Some feel morality compels them, others are wired differently and can't help but question the political arrangement of a civilization and the stories that organize it. Never mind the people who spent untold hours working to discover a new metal or invent a new pulley with no reason to continue but the vision inside their heads. 

Andrew Higgins, a hard-drinking, hot-tempered boatbuilder from New Orleans, spent years perfecting a flat-bottomed boat for the Louisiana swamps. In World War II, the Higgins boat was often made from plywood and carried Allied soldiers onto beaches in the Pacific and the Mediterranean, and most famously at Normandy.

And it's not just notable people. The fact that so many people will learn how to use available technologies and resources to build homes, teach kids, farm food, fix machines, process claims, all the while finding minute ways in their daily lives to advance civilization is amazing.

Different personalities can find success because diversity is ecosystem security. The fact most people share so much in common means we can still mass-produce the tools we need to populate the technological ecosystem. When World War II came, America turned to William Knudsen, a Danish immigrant who had risen from bicycle mechanic to president of General Motors. He left for a dollar a year to turn car factories into arsenals.

Diversity and mass production together are how genotype and phenotype found success in the biological world, and it’s the same recipe for the extended phenotype too. Biology grew to have countless species of trees, insects, birds, mammals, predators and so on. Thus evermore diverse human genotype and phenotype got a shot to play the game because of the need for diversity when co-evolving with tools. 

And that's the lesson of nature we need to embrace. While we have a genetic history forever in our DNA, our genes, personalities, and culture evolved along with technological change. Each society named these people by its own politics (queen, farmer, blacksmith, consumer), but every label sat on a deeper place in the energy web.

The technological evolution this book advocates has been driving human evolution for generations. In the technological ecosystem, people fill the same roles animals do: producers (farms, power plants), consumers (workers, homeowners, drivers), predators (innovators), scavengers (thrift buyers, auction goers), decomposers (recyclers, waste managers), and testers (tinkerers, hackers, and crooks). No coach pre-assigns these roles. The jobs are open, and someone always grows into them based on their mixture of genes, personality, and opportunity.

Note, these roles are older than any tool that fills them. Something has broken down dead matter for a billion years. Bacteria, fungi, beetles, and now auto wreckers and metal recyclers. The adaptation, job, identity, and tool have an expiry date. The role doesn't.

Producers grow the food or generate the electricity that powers technology. Consumers—most people most of the time—use tools to stay warm or cool, gather resources, raise their young.

Predators hunt inefficiency. Wherever a tool wastes time, energy, or material, someone eventually notices. The innovator isn’t attacking any person, but the tool that’s falling behind. Like any predator, they take what’s slowest and easiest to catch. When that prey is gone, the innovator moves to the next inefficiency. In World War II, American shipyards hunted every wasted hour out of building cargo ships, cutting the time from 1.4 million man-hours to 500,000. One, the _Robert E. Peary_, was launched in eight days.

Scavengers can't help but use, collect, and hoard every last piece of technology everyone else thinks is junk. Decomposers break down dead technology back into reusable inputs like wreckers recycling total loss cars.

Now for viruses, or what I like to call testers. These are the people who find novel uses for existing technology, just as viruses move genetic material between organisms. Sometimes the result is a helpful innovation, sometimes it's a crook—I dealt with plenty of those adjusting claims, and was accused of being one often enough to reflect on it. Either way, by constantly hacking whatever exists, testers check every member of the ecosystem for its fitness, acting as an ever-present selection pressure. (Testers isn't a standard ecological classification; I'm using it for simplicity.)

Notice that each role has its own relationship with time. Everything useful eventually runs down, so every player has to reach stored energy before the sand clock does, but each works at its own pace.

A producer, or farmer, waits patiently while sunlight slowly becomes growth. A consumer, or worker, wants a steady supply: daily forage, a regular paycheck. A predator, or innovator, may stalk for months for one big reward. A scavenger waits for whatever valuable thing turns up. A decomposer, or recycler, works constantly with whatever it gets. And a tester probes without pause, looking for the next weakness.

The sand clock ticks for everyone. How a player spends its time helps define its place in holding entropy back.

Let's look at each role in how streaming replaced Maria's DVD store. Producers converted energy into digital content, burned from hard drives onto plastic discs in the millions and trucked to stores. Consumers drove, picked up, watched, and satisfied their need for stories—a 20-minute round trip for two hours of content, hundreds of miles a year per family.

Innovators locked their eyes on this waste. They mutated the available material, digitized content, hard drives, home theaters, but cut out the physical discs. Instead they empowered people with high-speed internet to download from centralized hard drives.

These new tool owners were predators. They sought to replace the disc with smart TVs and streaming sticks the DVD stores never recognized as a threat. It worked. Every increase in download speed eliminated a little more of the DVD's user base, because the drive, the late fee, and the empty shelf on Friday night were like air drag on the wings of a bird. Hackers tested the old system by making illegal downloads available when streaming wasn't. The result was people and their tools became more aerodynamic, more efficient, with every download. 

Producers switched from making DVDs to making data center hard drives and fiber-optic cables. Demo teams smashed the closed stores and warehouses and collected the spare computers, trucks, and shipping equipment. This material and any excess merchandise was auctioned off to whoever could scavenge the last bit of value from the meat of the dead DVD industry.

Maria's customers weren't stolen from her. They watched their stories with a faster set of adaptations, as a new technological species. The energy flow biologists study so well happens with human roles too. The energy saved built faster networks, data centers, and computers that enabled—among other things—the music videos, instruction videos, and documentaries. When Maria lost her regional manager identity the saved energy benefited the ecosystem enormously.

Like the toy hammers and blocks under a Christmas tree, the things we play with often become the tools that evolve us. Streaming drove demand for faster home internet, which later carried telemedicine and work from anywhere. Graphics chips built for video games now power AI.

The need to invent, power, and manually operate tools changed how humans were selected. Where biological ecosystems' roles are often captured by a dedicated species, ours are captured by people with dedicated temperaments: the hoarder who can't throw anything away, the innovator who can't leave a slow tool alone, the tinkerer who breaks things to see how they work. Like omnivores, we can each be a mix, of course, but together our personalities cover every job in the food web's energy flow. That's how, even though we were leaving the biological ecosystem behind, we were building another technological one beside it.

Humans were no longer just hunting and gathering what nature made available, but instead creating and operating a new ecosystem in a modified Game of Life. Civilized humans kept scoring more points. More food, housing, and fresh water expanded the board’s carrying capacity, and populations grew from Stone to Bronze to Iron Age and beyond.

Some might consider these changes artificial. After all, stone walls, bronze swords, horse saddles, and everything else in civilization are man-made materials or objects.

But that would be wrong. Why is a copper water pipe or medical textbook artificial if the copper in our bodies or the wood in decomposing trees are natural? The Game of Life is a battle to grow things that can self-replicate in their environment faster than they degrade. As physicist Richard Muller notes in his book "Now: The Physics of Time", technology does that every bit as much as biology does.

That's why projects that demand sameness—whether by race, ideology, or class—work against the technological ecosystem. They cull the people that would fill the many niches of a healthy ecosystem. They don't just constrain individual freedom; they starve the ecosystem of the players it requires to sustain itself against the true enemy; the great darkness that never stops. Entropy.

The problem is technology does not always grow as naturally as we need it to keep playing the game.

## Story—Help

Adjusting insurance claims was genuinely stressful. But also rewarding. Insurance sets money aside for people in need, so you get to help when people need it most.

The system could be nuts, the job wasn't fun, but the people's needs were very real.

Someone's car was stolen, or there was a hit-and-run, sometimes even a drive-by shooting. If it's covered (those usually are with comprehensive and collision coverage), you help get their car fixed or replaced.

Adjusters work for the insurer, the “second party” and the insured is the “first party”. The policy requires insureds to answer relevant questions. That's what makes the work so educational: we step into a customer's situation and must either vouch for their story or do a documented investigation to disprove it (a tall order but it happens sometimes).

This was especially true for two claims I can't forget.

One coworker was given a pile of claims to investigate after a large flood. He got to one high-end home, asked to see the water damage, and told the homeowner to send in their damaged items list for review. This was the first time I learned about "cat" claims—as in catastrophe.

Another was a call I got after 5:00 PM on the emergency call-in queue. It was a young single mother. The waste-water pipes had burst in her walls. I was still just a car adjuster, but procedure was to send restoration contractors over to contain the damage; a property adjuster would later review if insurance covered the bill.

I told her that, but the lady refused the repairmen.

I ignored her protest—she had sewage all over her home, this obviously needed to be fixed regardless. I continued filling out the needed forms. She said, "No!" _Insureds are so frustrating_, I told myself.

Then she said, "I don't have any money."

I quickly googled her area. She was in the poor area of a poor town. And I had just forced her to admit she had nothing.

It was already past working hours, meaning she'd likely get an insurance answer within a few days. I became a property adjuster later on and learned she had insurance coverage for her problem. She had the money, but didn't have it at the same time.

I often wonder what that mother did while she waited, with sewage in the walls, nothing in the bank, and a kid in the house. I hope she had somewhere to go and friends to call on. But I'd seen enough by then to know those were luxuries for too many people.

As for the high-end homeowner—he noticed my coworker had forgotten to photograph parts of his basement. A bare basement, empty of almost anything. He submitted his item list months later. Antique furniture and priceless art, supposedly worth hundreds of thousands, if not millions.

His case in court was simple: "You were there. Prove I didn't have it." The case went on for years. I'm pretty sure he eventually won too much money.

Time told us who really needed the help. But the system couldn’t tell the difference between desperation and greed. We had to treat everyone the same, even though one person’s need made the work worthwhile, and another’s greed made it crap.

## Chapter 11—Back to Natural

When our ancestors lost their fur, they borrowed it from dead animals and worked to master fire. The day we first cooked over a fire we’d started ourselves, biology gave birth to technology.

Sparking a flame cooked our food, got us warm, and fed our big brains to think up ways to keep us going. Soon, the fire inside kilns and forges turned clay into pottery and minerals into metals. Fire made pots, nails, knives, and parts of civilization.

But there's nothing supernatural about technology. As we saw, fire runs on oxygen life made to fuel its organization of clay, copper, zinc, and other natural elements into useful organization of matter and is thus a natural part of the Game of Life.

What became artificial is the method used to select the tools of our ecosystems.

The first brown bears venturing into the Arctic didn't interview for jobs. Today's brown bears can't protest against polar bears venturing south looking for meals. They can only play the game and vie to be naturally selected to keep playing it. But when our big brains, self-awareness, and storytelling mix with biological self-interest to be as lazy as possible—especially while holding powerful tools—the result can be unnatural.

We see each other not as technological operators in the ecosystem that sustains us, but as tribe members or outsiders, sorted by nation, ethnicity, ideology, and other old labels. We too often try to subvert the natural reciprocation boardmates must make. The bear can't empty the coast, the seal can't evade all bears. An equilibrium forms among all the roles.

Human civilization has no built-in limits to the actions of its players. Biological ecosystems have so many niches for each producer, scavenger, decomposer, etc. But we often shut people out for ideological, religious, or other reasons, so they can’t fill an untapped niche. That doesn’t move their energy to you; it removes it from the game board, leaving less energy to flip the sand clock.

Empowering an ecosystem pays well. The more trees, the more deer, the more predators, the more scavengers, the more decomposers, the more soil means the more matter organized. Give a boardmate a better tool and their harvest grows, which grows the ecosystem you both feed on. That is the whole difference between the two kinds of artificial selection, the phenomenon we saw in the Heike crab. One version empowers other lifeforms, the other feeds on them, and only one of them can last.

Shutting people out is costly. It interferes with the flow of energy and deprives the ecosystem of the benefits of full staffing. For most of our history we still spent much of our time living, harvesting, and depending on the bounty biological ecosystem provided.

However, in 2020, researchers calculated something remarkable.

Such is the success of the fire and humans that the total weight of everything the two have created—buildings, vehicles, infrastructure, plastic, concrete—now exceeds the total weight of all living biomass on Earth. The entire biological world is often called the biosphere. In that light, there is now more organized matter in the technosphere than in the environment that birthed it.

When technology is powered by electricity, built with robots, designed with AI, and outweighs all life put together, we can't keep pretending our primary identity is rooted in the genetics of ancient humans or the cultures of the past.

Said in another way, what are the odds that your self-identity is matched with your unique time and place on this new game board? 

The technosphere is entirely new and rapidly changing.

How could Maria have known her future? How could any of us know, when none of us has been here before? We've had so many identities between generations, ages, ideologies, and none have been made in this new environment.

And now we have AI. In the past we had fire, kilns, forges, and the future is who knows. 

Every time a new technology arrives, we know the feeling of joy and dread. But like a raccoon who's learned to discern the noises of a busy restaurant, we don't really understand why the trends of technology come and go. As animals shed their fur in spring, we must shed our tools but until we embrace a new identity rooted in the energy web of organized technologies, we won't really know who we are. We'll be forced to act within old identities while building and maintaining order in the eternal Game of Life on this new game board.

Thinkers have compared technology to evolution before. But no political or economic system we live under has used evolutionary thinking to select its tools.

Tool ecosystems, like biological ones, grow, speciate, flow, mutate, drift, and follow the other tenets of Darwinian evolution. Tools play on the same game board, and must use local resources to procreate more often to populate the land. The rise and fall of countries, peoples, nations, and empires tracks this evolution more closely than we like to admit because it's a fate that affects us so much, yet we don't have a name or identity for it.

I can guarantee house fires, tornado damage, car crashes don't take human stories into consideration. The pain these acts of rising entropy cause is the plot creatures evolve to solve. The squirrel must plant trees to have them and the shelter they provide. The tree must make nutritious nuts to get planted each year. There's no self-awareness of course, but the byproduct is there so none is needed.

We have self-awareness, so we must aim at the things we need to prosper. In both Games of Life, solving real pains is the way to win. Every delay restoring a forest or a house keeps one more player, a squirrel, a welder, a trucker, needlessly off the board.

The biggest difference in technological evolution is artificial selection by human storytelling, beliefs, and ideologies. Where natural selection constantly picks new species for the changing environment, we think we can do better. Sometimes we can as seen in bountiful harvests and widespread prosperity, but sometimes we foul it up seen with economic recessions, depressions, and even wars as our tools can't harvest the resources we need to keep rising disorder away.

The last century twice demonstrated the complete failure of human storytelling to control our fate. We met both attempts already: fascism and communism, each convinced it had found the engine of human success, each bound to fail at enormous cost because it was an isolated half of evolution.

Tracing their history shows us the definition our new identity must have. A one filled with moderation and compromise between genotype and phenotype, sorely missing today.

One belief system looked at all boardmates and saw only genotype: each person is solely defined by their bloodline, so purge the dead weight and their superiors will advance civilization to untold heights. The other saw only phenotype: remake the human condition by empowering educated decision-makers to engineer a society to make everyone—unless they are tainted by their past—a better human.

Both believed they had cracked the code of human advancement. Pre-screen boardmates to create a common identity. Sold from stages and newsreels, at marches and conferences, each promised that one shared identity would make people cooperate more and raise the carrying capacity for everyone.

Both implemented their ideas, and the world watched as evolution judged the results within a lifetime.

## Chapter 12—Extremes Are Not Natural

Both the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany put their ideas into practice at full scale, on real populations, at the same time. Neither called it a test. Both were certain enough to build countries and new world orders on the answers their belief systems gave them.

Nazi beliefs attributed human success and failure to genetic heredity alone. Destiny was sealed at conception, unreachable, and legible in a person's body. The fact that many of Europe's best minds—among the long list is Albert Einstein, Leo Szilard, and Hans Bethe, John von Neumann—fled, or didn't return, for their apparent unworthiness dissuaded no believer. After all, Germany had many engineers, singers, artists, and scientists who worked inside a regime while it measured skulls to rank intelligence and traced ancestry to sniff out supposed undesirables.

Soviet science made the opposite wager. Genetics was a myth, it was the environment that made a lifeform. So, they revived Lamarckism; an old idea modern genetics had displaced. Soviet officials even attacked genetics by name as "Weismannism" after the barrier we met in Chapter 7. This theory said heredity could change based on environmental factors. Meaning for example, we can "teach" wheat to flower on different seasons and its offspring will inherit this new adaptation. When respected Soviet genetic researchers like Nikolai Koltsov and Nikolai Vavilov rightfully disagreed they were persecuted or killed. By 1948 genetic research itself was banned. By imposing this state doctrine there was a supposed scientific proof that any plant or person would prosper in any environment, if only it were properly conditioned for it.

These were two entire societies based on promoting, and demoting, either the genotype or phenotype part of human identity. Both thought they found the secrets to winning the Game of Life. They wanted to capture new scientific and technological achievements. New factories, research labs, armaments foundries were founded and funded throughout both regimes with the emblems adorning them to showcase their patron's wisdom.

However, as awful as it might sound, these extremes could have some success. Despite their prejudices, both had the people to employ new machinery, mathematics, and other advancements to build modern economies and militaries. Yet each was doomed. Both extremes had intellectual complacency embedded in them and could never sustain any success in the Game of Life. And this failure shows us the moderate mix our new identity must have.

Let's showcase why by first looking at World War II (1939-1945). This history is told in many books (my favorite being _Why the Allies Won_), but here's the short version from the German perspective. (This history runs longer than most in the book, to hopefully help dissuade anyone who might still be seduced by extremes today.)

It starts in the previous war. German strategy in 1914 aimed at defeating France quickly by invading through Belgium, then turning east to fight Russia. Being in the middle of Europe and easy to blockade, Germany wanted to avoid a long two-front war that would strain its resources.

World War I was the first great war between industrialized powers. Historically, farm labor was manual and much of a country's population was bound to the land for planting and harvests. From the late 1800s onwards, tractors and other mechanizations started to free labor hours. With increased productivity, farms could supply enough food, populations boomed, and now more soldiers could battle year round.

Mechanization didn't stop at the farm. World War I was also the first time industrial powers used machine guns, high explosives, and artillery against each other at a total-war scale. Old battlefields would only be miles long with hundreds of thousands of men concentrated together using swords, lances, or relatively slow muskets. Generals saw battles unfold and gave orders in near-realtime. Now battlefields were thousands of miles of trenches and bunkers with millions of men, machine guns, and high-explosives and leaders were miles behind the action trying to make sense of it all.

The point is, despite German need for a quick war, they found out their old battle tactics could not maneuver with so many men holding repeating rifles, machine guns, and artillery. Soldiers thus attacked each other in waves, protected by endless artillery barrages, and relaying observations to commanders far away from the battlefield. It was a war of attrition, so both sides spent years increasing inputs—more men, bullets, explosive shells—trying to overpower the enemy's defenses.

Every leader mourned their dead, tried different tactics, but few saw any alternatives to the trenches, poison gas, shell shock, and horror.

Enter Australian World War I general John Monash. He was a part-time general before the war but a full-time engineer. He saw a way through the carnage. Monash was a predator of inefficiency, in the sense of Chapter 10: he hunted the waste in wave attacks. He trained his soldiers, tanks, and planes to support each other based on the unique problems they faced. Instead of sending wave attacks to overwhelm a defender's trench, Monash had his planes bomb a vulnerable section, just before tanks approached it, so that riflemen can attack an enemy not sure where to shoot. So, no more increasing inputs with wave attacks. Instead soldiers would be an orchestra of different tools that learned to play the same sheet music, i.e. battle plans. The mentality was called "combined arms warfare".

Monash's ideas culminated in the Battle of Hamel (1918). In just over an hour and a half, Monash did what previous offensives had failed to do in months of bloody attrition. Hamel was a masterpiece of planning. Every gun, tank, aircraft and rifleman worked to a single synchronized timetable. Armor crushed the machine-gun nests, a creeping barrage shielded the infantry, and aircraft parachuted ammunition directly to the front line.

The result was a clear victory with comparatively few Allied losses. It showed that technology and meticulous planning could save lives and break the stalemate. A month later at Amiens and the 100 Days Campaign, the same method helped turn the tide of the war.

But months later a starving Germany surrendered. The many other Allied generals, who at-first resisted even trying it, used some of its tactics to finish the war, but never adopted Monash's methods as a new doctrine. Instead, victory over Germany was credited to strong bunkers and defensive strategy the Allies had used through four years of war. Combined arms ideas were discussed and considered interesting. British theorists even developed war games and wrote books about it. The Allied leadership read them, but never implemented the doctrine to any meaningful degree.

In contrast, World War I taught every German that they were correct. Germany's encircled geography means it cannot win long wars of attrition. From the top generals to the common soldier, no German had reason to preserve World War I mentalities. So, predating the old tools and tactics was in vogue. They developed some of the h"storm trooper" tactics that had proven successful. When they read the British theorists, they liked the idea of being far more efficient with their military. General Heinz Guderian wrote and distributed a practical guide to combined arms warfare called _Beware the Tank! (1937)_.

The German military took the book to heart. They practiced concentrating many trucks, tanks, planes, and artillery like a sword point at the weak points in the enemy's forces. More importantly, using mobile radios, all soldiers could communicate with other units and shared intelligence and commands very fast. Whereas Allied soldiers still relayed orders to generals far behind the frontlines and waited hours or days for needed supplies or reinforcements, German dive-bombers, tanks, mobile artillery, and motorized infantry arranged attacks on enemy positions together, at the same time, on the frontline. Germans called combined arms warfare "war of movement", western journalists were soon to call it "Blitzkrieg", or lightning warfare.

The Germans didn't invent modern technology. Meaning, new and powerful planes, trucks, tanks, and radios were transforming societies around the globe. The Allies after World War I researched and built these modern weapons just like the Germans did. But Allied leaders owed their careers to World War I methods. They were content on consuming the tactics, tools, and technologies as they already understood them during the war.

The result was no further predation. Instead, combined arms ideas were filtered out with every training session and promotion. That's how the Allied armies trained themselves not to self-reflect and see the environment—and as a result they made an echo chamber. Radios, tanks, and airplanes were purchased and then added to each unit like a new portable kitchen: nice to have, but not pivotal. Each tool was spread thin, often behind bunkers, connected by layers of commanders relaying orders and assuming there would be time to act before anything bad happened. After all, "moving" the dense frontlines of World War I trenches took weeks of broad wave attacks.

Then in September 1939 Germany invaded Poland. The die was cast as the sequel to World War I had started.

The Poles fought hard for weeks, but when the Soviets invaded them from the other side, the contest was never in question. The Germans would then take Denmark and Norway with relative ease. When Germany turned its Blitzkrieg on its World War I foes Belgium, Britain, and France in May 1940, the Nazi's truly tested its new military.

An early obstacle was the massive Belgian Fort Eben-Emael. It was built to protect several important bridges that controlled the Belgian heartland from invasion. Made from all the lessons of World War I, it was a massive underground city with concrete protected machine guns and artillery pieces. 

Belgian defenders waited for German tanks, artillery, and soldiers to attack. Instead the Germans landed gliders on the fort's roof. Without even being detected, 85 troopers walked out, used newly developed shaped explosive charges to blast open the concrete gun emplacements, and the fortress fell in under two days. With barely a struggle, German tanks rolled on and Belgium fell just a few weeks later.

Fort Eben-Emael was symbolic of the entire Allied battle plan. The French built bunkers that were far larger, the biggest called the Maginot Line, and none of them prevented invasion. Instead of landing on them like in Eben-Emael, the Germans' massed tanks drove around them, and faced surprised and disorganized Allied soldiers and officers trained to expect the long timelines and the massive and predictable wave assaults of World War I. 

For the Allied societies and militaries, suddenly facing concentrated planes, tanks, motorized machine guns, mobile artillery, all working directly together was a new reality and a complete mental shock. It was like cheating—if such a thing could exist in war. The Allied people wanted peace, their militaries were sure repeating their defensive bunker tactics would bring it. Yet, the German Blitzkrieg burst through the shocked Allied lines and surrounded the confused Allied armies from all directions. Cut off and isolated, the Allied units gradually surrendered. In six weeks the Blitzkrieg did what four years of horrendously bloody war couldn't, and conquered most of Western Europe.

One French officer summed up the trauma with a postcard. It was addressed to the country's leader and found on his body, "I am killing myself Mr. President to let you know that all my men were brave, but one cannot send men to fight tanks with rifles."

The Nazis had just beaten an enemy who refused to adapt. The Allies not only originated combined arms warfare, they watched Germany train it, publish books about it, and watched it work so well against Poland, Denmark, and Norway. And they still didn't change their war plans. The military leaders had the order backwards. They assumed leaders are the most qualified to assess the environment. Instead, it's the best at assessing the environment who should be the leaders because that's what defines a successful species in the Game of Life. This is artificial selection at work. The Allies promoted officers who fit the story of the last war, not the board in front of them.

And, here is the point so important to us, almost right away the Nazis made the same mistake, and their inherent extremism had them inevitably make this mistake. Despite matching better tactics to the environment and getting the victory it wanted in World War I (they even signed the 1940 armistice in the same train car as in 1918, even going so far to return it to the same spot), they decided to keep fighting. They didn't assess their new environment, instead they thought genetically superior leaders didn't need to.

The Nazis read their early wins as proof of their ideology. The fact anyone can and did emulate their tools and tactics passed them by. Blitzkrieg wasn't even all that original. Two military historians explained Napoleon's 19th century warfare tactics as "Napoleonic Blitzkrieg". Like Monash a century later, Napoleon was engineer-minded who was very adept at using modern technology of horses, mobile cannons, canned food, and army corps. Studying his campaigns military theorist Jomini called his doctine one that concentrated overwhelming forces at the "point of decision".

Moreover, surprising a foe and attacking his vulnerable points is hardly unnatural. We know how much nature selects those who are lazy while getting the job done. In many respects, combined arms warfare just re-applied long standing principles with new technology.

Nevertheless, with Western Europe under their grip, the Nazis assumed the "racially inferior" Soviets would be a push-over, evidenced by the Soviet Union's recent purge of tens of thousands of its officers and lackluster performance in the Winter War against Finland (1939-1940). Although the German invasion of the Soviet Union (1941) caused an egregious loss of Soviet life and property it failed to force a surrender or capture much needed oil fields. Within a few years the Soviets were fielding better and more reliable tanks and planes and approaching German military command sophistication. By 1943 the tide of the war turned for the Soviets never to swing back again.

And the Allies woke up. Charles de Gaulle had spent the 1930s writing that France needed concentrated armored divisions. As we've seen already these ideas were widely ignored. But when the Germans proved him right, the man nobody had listened to became the voice of Free France. Britain, having left its army equipment in France, rebuilt around mobile formations and put its faith in radar and codebreaking—the latter of which its prewar leadership had barely funded. Britain and France now promoted the people and tools they had previously filtered out.

But the biggest change came when America entered the war in late 1941.

With "Rosie the Riveter" and "Victory Program" leading the way, America turned what the Nazis called a decadent consumer economy to mass-produce quality and reliable weapons, planes, ships faster than anyone thought possible. William Knudsen, the machinist who went on to lead General Motors, was promoted to General and given authority to direct Army's war production.

It worked wonders. In 1942 America already outproduced all enemy states combined, 47,000 aircraft to 27,000, 24,000 tanks to 11,000, and six times as many heavy guns. The disparity only increased as time went on.

America turned a 190,000 man pre-war army into over 8,000,000. Army Chief of Staff George Marshall ran war games to see which generals needed removing, and removed them. Soon new commanders adept at modern warfare like Patton, Bradley, and Eisenhower were in power, while American generals being rotated into/out of commands based on their battlefield performance became expected and routine.

This is natural predation, but also self-aware selection: choosing leaders, tools, and workers on what the environment actually rewards, and changing course when results say so.

Vicotry took priority over anything else. The American war effort even began to crack open long-held sexist and racist policies. Women filled factories, and Black soldiers, still kept in segregated units, drove most of the trucks of the renowned Red Ball Express that brought supplies to soldiers after D-Day. The diversity of participation, involvement, and ideas helped keep the American war economy adapting.

With a mass-producing homefront, assigning leadership based on merit, and willingness to  open up its social structures, America started doing combined arms warfare far better than Germany ever did. Soon, liberation came to North Africa, Sicily and Italy, then France, Belgium, Holland, and finally Germany.

This is not to say victory was assured. As the author of _Why the Allies Won_ notes, in the middle years, the "conflict was poised on a knife’s edge". The point being made here was that Nazi Germany, so obsessed with genetic explanations for its successes, couldn't respond outside of its own extreme beliefs.

These beliefs were increasingly self-defeating. They instilled fear in all their neighbors, even those supporting them like Romania and Hungary. While the average German soldier was famously skilled, and radicals like the Waffen-SS fought very hard, many soldiers were checking out. Hundreds of thousands of dissenting German soldiers were imprisoned and an estimated 15,000 executed for desertion (for reference America executed one deserter). Nazis adopted women as workers, but never embraced mobilizing their full potential despite their encircled geography once again making natural resources and manpower very limited.

While some Nazi leaders worked to increase weapons production (which did increase), the regime's economy was obsessed with showcasing German superiority by making high-quality and ingenious weapons. While the famous Panzer tanks, Messerschmitt jet fighter, and V2 rockets attract viewers in today's museums and documentaries, in war these weapons were frequently unreliable, hard to mass-produce, and had a showroom polish no frontline soldier cared about.

For an example, we could go further into America's Higgins landing craft or Liberty cargo ships. But instead, let's take a look at the unglamorous cargo trucks. Every army needs constant bullets, food, and fuel because as Napoleon is said to have proclaimed "An army marches on its stomach". America put five truck models into production. Germany had 151. It later cut down to 23 to increase production levels, but it never came close to America's ability to make a truck. Most other German tools had similarly needless complexity. Their supply chain was a logistical nightmare of incompatible parts, which partly explains why horses still pulled most of German army supplies throughout the war. For all its planning and reading of _Beware the Tank!_, Germany could equip only a small fraction of its military for Blitzkrieg.

Yet while lagging far behind its enemies in the production of planes, tanks, and ships, the regime kept hunting supposed undesirables for its multiple ongoing genocides. It also lost the people it might have won over. East Europeans who had every reason to hate the Soviet Union for its labor camps and famines were treated even worse by the Nazis, and soon fought against their hopeful liberators.

Instead of adapting, the Nazis increased their brutality and waited for a self-declared German genius to deliver "wunderweapons" that would save them—never realizing their own outcasts were building the first computers, radar, and atomic bombs in Allied countries.

As their conquests were liberated one by one, nothing changed, even at the end. Contrary to their doctrine of German nobility, the leadership did not die fighting alongside their soldiers. Most hid, fled, or killed themselves. As the Thousand-Year Reich was crumbling they wrote that the German nation had failed the racial test of history. Beating bunkers had made their reputation. Now they died in their own, to avoid any evidence that it was Nazi beliefs that had failed the nation.

After six years of war, the Allies and the Soviets met over the ruins of Germany and divided Europe between them. One extreme was gone. The other had just been handed half a continent. Extremism of the genotype had failed, and now we get to see how extremism of the phenotype is no better.

In 1953 the Soviet Union began to moderate its extreme policies with de-Stalinization. The country's generals, leaders, and people no longer feared mass executions  or famines. But the country could never moderate its core belief: that properly trained persons are selfless, and therefore personal ambition is always bad.

There were technological triumphs for the Soviets to celebrate. To America's great surprise, they launched Sputnik, the world's first satellite (1957), then achieved more breakthroughs with the first experimental fusion reactors (1958) and sending probes to Venus (1966-1983). But Soviet progress stalled for the same reason a Soviet biologist could not study genetic theory. The state had to police each person's thoughts and actions, and therefore controlled the laboratories, the military, and everything else.

When state doctrine is infused everywhere, individuals with different ideas are out of luck. As a result, Soviet society had no "people in a garage," no small businesses, no independent labor unions, no independent universities, no non-sanctioned ambition. 

The Soviet leaders' job was to protect society from these exploitive individuals, especially by removing anyone's access to private property. Because their doctrine said when the most important tools and land are controlled by educated persons, the result would be a classless communist utopia.

With so much responsibility vested in leadership, their actions were shielded from reproach. And, contrary to their doctrine, but faithful to the human nature they wanted to correct, leadership acted selfishly. They withheld their society's many military and scientific developments from reaching the tools regular people used. Despite fusion reactors and going to the Moon and Venus, TVs, calculators, toilet paper didn't change much through Soviet decades.

Without independent research, free enterprise, or private property there was nothing anyone could do about it. The result was a society with little "creative destruction." Instead, government ministries tried to plan the entire economy years in advance. So, ball-bearings, pen clips, rubber tires, everything was given production quotas. The result was factories got too many of one component and too few of another, resulting in store shelves chronically empty of useful products.

With so little being produced, everyone knew the best jobs were rising in the government hierarchy. The classless society was therefore self-policing. Opposing ideas were filtered out with each new promotion and training session, especially because in a society without free commerce, control over state property was the only currency. The system could correct itself—genetics was allowed again after 1964—but instead of investigating nature, trying new things, and seeing what worked everyone learned to wait for instructions.

In contrast, when America put rockets in space, average citizens were following along in real-time on radios and TVs sharing the same technology. As Soviet innovation stalled in the 1970s, the Soviet Union grew ever more dependent on oil exports to fund a bloated state bureaucracy and military. It needed to buy the advanced industrial equipment, and even food, it could not produce itself. Regular workers joked "we pretend to work, they pretend to pay us". When video tapes of bountiful western supermarkets arrived on Soviet home television, it pierced through government propaganda of supposed communist plenty. Then oil prices fell in the 1980s, and the regime could not afford to import the little it already had. Soon, the entire Soviet system imploded as its various peoples either voted to abandon it or watched as even once-committed believers let the communist project die.

Both extremist regimes hit the same wall because they misplayed the same game. They both wanted to mix scientific advancement and mass-production to create amazing new tools. Both thought screening boardmates would make that happen and ended up erasing the that fuels involvement and new ideas needed to keep creating stuff worth mass-producing.

Neither saw technology as an ecosystem of producers, consumers, predators, etc. who need all types to operate it. Thus, each regime's carrying capacity was far below what it had promised its believers. Instead of reflecting, both resorted to increasing inputs—by taking, or by selling. Germany needed Austrian gold, Czech factories, Ukrainian grain, and Caucasus oil. The Soviets seized many German and Japanese power stations and factories after the war, innovated on that equipment for a time, then had to sell oil to stay afloat until even that wasn't enough.

People selecting winners and losers is not itself a problem. Every farmer selects, every teacher selects, every coach selects. The problem was what the extremists selected on. Class origin and bloodline aren't measures of fitness; they're faces in a crab shell.

As we learned in the Game of Life, nature keeps score on something else entirely: adaptations that fill an ecological role, feeding and sustaining the others. Both regimes promised that pre-screening boardmates would remove conflict and raise carrying capacity for everyone. It did the opposite. The people they screened out were the ones they needed. No genotype or phenotype extreme can operate an entire ecosystem alone.

Nature would have reached the same verdict eventually. Wars terrible, but so are new diseases, crop blights, and environmental catastrophes. Players don’t get to decide what the game board and sand clock will bring tomorrow. Solar flares or supernovas, wars or ice ages have tested life in the pas and will continue into the future.

Self-aware selection sees changes coming and responds on purpose. It’s far better to select on the board, not on the story.

## Story—Winter Is Coming

"Yes, you must come into the office. It's a work day."

I was chagrined and befuddled. Car crashes were sure to come. I knew that because I was standing in two feet of rising snow and ice.

Nonetheless, I finished shoveling, got into my tiny sedan, and set off.

I'd processed claims during winter storms before, but this was the first time I was fairly sure I'd be filing a claim of my own.

I made it out of my local roadway. Driving on a collector road, I was on the wrong side of the unplowed street. I had no choice because that's where the tracks led in the deep-snow. Stopping meant getting stuck. Turning the wheel meant skidding. 

I kept thinking about what would happen if a plow came this way.

It didn't. I reached the main arterial road, which was plowed often and was relatively cleared. I trickled along congested roads and highways, and finally got to an office building. The claims bell was dinging all day. There wasn't much we could actually do. Tow truck services, rental companies, and repair shops were already overwhelmed.

We answered calls, sent emails, forwarded approvals as fast as we could. Most insureds understood there would be delays from tow trucks to rental cars. Winter storms come every year, after all. 

The snow kept falling. We were sent home early to try to get back safely. I drove two co-workers home. I was proud I didn't get stuck.

I can't recall how many claims I handled that day. Hundreds of thousands of dollars in damage and several injuries at least. It made me think of something.

I loved playing strategy games and city builders as a kid. There was something enjoyable about managing resources and building an environment that fosters a happy life. It's why I enrolled in city planning at university.

I also worked through school. One of those jobs was analyzing ads in business-to-business magazines. After a year there, I was allowed to work from home four days a week. I slept an extra hour each day, my grades improved, I saved much-needed cash, and I exercised more. The exact same work got done.

I happily mentioned this to my transportation professor. After all, broadband internet was saturating the country. He dismissed it: "We tried this a decade ago and managers didn't like it." He went back to teaching traffic control management: timed lights, coordinated signals, variable message signs.

What's better for traffic management than fewer vehicles on the road? Especially in snowstorms or energy shocks. But impossible, apparently, because remote doesn't work. A funny opinion because my job was soon outsourced to another continent and that raised no objections from anyone.

Partly because of that class, I switched my specialty to history and political theory. A player cannot defer problems in civilization simulation games. There's no blame to reassign or cans to kick. The player must make choices based on the resources at hand, like figuring out what to do when snow blankets roads or energy costs spike.

We either make those hard choices or let them overtake us. A person committed to a toolset sees fewer cars as a threat. But a player who sees the ecosystem sees a solution that frees energy to spend elsewhere. 

And, who has an interest in preventing entropy more than insurance?

When I started my insurance job, I went back to my alma mater and found that in one of the biggest libraries in the world, insurance took up a single section of a single stack. But there was a gem: _Insuring the Industrial Revolution_ by Robin Pearson. It's a very well researched book on how insurance helped Britain industrialize as fast as it did.

The Great Fire of London (1666) gave birth to fire insurance. Early insurers even ran their own fire brigades, which only put out fires at buildings bearing their company’s mark. As cities kept burning (Edinburgh, Newcastle, Chicago, Boston, Toronto, San Francisco), insurers paid to rebuild them and pushed for firefighters, fire escapes, extinguishers, and drills.

Surely the industry that paid for car crashes would want fewer car crashes. Yet the bell was still dinging. And we kept driving year round, regardless of the traffic, weather, cost of fuel, countless crashes, and injuries.

Where's the return on the energy we spent putting screens, sound systems, and broadband into every home? We built the theater and kept driving to the office anyway. It's like plumbing every house and still requiring everyone to walk to the public baths.

A player of the game would see the ecosystem and make other choices.

## Chapter 13—Moderation Is Natural

The democracies that defeated Nazi Germany and later outlasted the Soviet Union, collectively known as the West, were different. Not by intent, but by structure. They had founding stories to guide their politics, but these never declared themselves to be perfect so were more moderate and open to change.

The power to create and modify society and technology was placed in many hands. Legislatures, courts, states, churches, unions, universities, private companies, and other institutions each held a piece, and none held enough to disregard a whole category of persons or ideas. No single story could screen out a whole kind of player, so the board kept both halves of evolution in play: mass production to make more of what works, and diversity to keep finding what might work next.

Benjamin Franklin showcased this moderation very well. One of the Founding Fathers of America, he was a printer by trade, and invented the lightning rod, bifocals, and a more efficient stove. But he refused to patent them, reasoning that since we all benefit from others’ inventions, we should gladly share our own. He also helped found a lending library, a volunteer fire company, and America’s first successful fire insurer. He achieved all this because in the country he helped found, he never needed to appease one strict ideology or one authority’s blessing.

Sadly, exclusion and oppression were real in the West, and often brutal. Government overreach existed. Businesses could be very selfish. But nothing was ever total, because there was another jurisdiction, another employer, another lab. Western boardmates were more free to play the game, and so often had to make better deals with each other: a worker making a better product, a business giving a pay raise, or a bureaucrat writing a useful regulation. Boardmates who didn’t reciprocate soon found the other had moved on to a different product, employer, or jurisdiction

Complacency certainly happened, the interwar Western militaries being one large example. But when reality hit, like with Blitzkrieg and later Sputnik, the West's distributed structure responded faster and better than its extremist adversaries could. 

In short, moderation let life “find a way.” More of the technological ecosystem’s open jobs got filled, from producers to predators, consumers to scavengers. More energy got harvested, and the board could support more tools, and more kinds of them, than anywhere else.

The system was by no means perfect. The many civil rights marches, environmental protests, and contested elections, showcase that well enough. But friction is a feature not a bug. The West often looked messier, but that was because problems were actually being addressed instead of assuming ideological belief made them go away. In 1947 former British Prime Minister Churchill even joked, "democracy is the worst form of Government except for all those other forms that have been tried from time to time". That is a joke impossible to tell in many extremist societies, never mind hear a celebrated wartime leader say it.

So while never perfect, multi-party democracy with free-market economics was less blinding and therefore more responsive to game board changes. It's why as time went on, most other extremist regimes—from Spain to Vietnam—adopted some or all of its more liberal market-based practices. Observers had learned, sometimes an arrogant inventor really does have exceptional gifts. Sometimes a self-righteous educated bureaucrat should regulate society. Neither extreme was wrong about everything, which is exactly why neither genotype nor phenotype should be discarded.

As biology shows many times over, an organism is a mixture of good genes and the right lessons on how to use them. An ecosystem is a mixture of these organisms, because different characteristics are needed to fill different niches. Conservatives tend to value inherited tradition and the instinct to mass-produce what works; progressives value diversity and want to embrace what's new. Both are describing something valuable.

Each extremist regime kept one side and thereby lost the benefits of one half of evolution. Having both halves didn't guarantee the West the better answer. But its distributed structure kept them moderating each other, which made the better answer easier to find. 

So the real question is how does moderation produce new tools consistently. We know the answer quite well actually. The man who ran America's wartime science effort spent the rest of his life explaining and building it.

You're likely holding the result in your hand.

In 1945, a technical genius envisioned the world we live in today. Before anyone sent an email or browsed Wikipedia, American engineer and inventor Dr. Vannevar Bush thought it up. He was running America's wartime science effort, and in _As We May Think_ he dreamed technology would soon help scientists organize, link, and retrieve the explosion of human knowledge he was witnessing.

Visionaries like Bush are hard to come by. Yet they exist in every society. The key is how to bring their ideas to life.

We’ve seen that a healthy ecosystem needs every kind of boardmate: the ambitious and the generous, the mass producer and the oddball. The transistor shows it better than any theory.

This tiny device switches on and off according to what we program it to do. It's what a cell is to biology, and the more assembled together the more capable the collection becomes. Each one turns a speck of sand into technomass that computes and perhaps even thinks.

The transistor was born from a partnership no single ideology contains: government demand, private enterprise, open research, and open market fused into one pipeline. Vannevar Bush embodied this pipeline in so many ways. In addition to being America's wartime science chief, he was the dean of MIT School of Engineering, and a Raytheon founder. He even described how to fund and integrate this society wide system of invention with his report to the president, _Science: the Endless Frontier_ (1945).

The transistor showcases this pipeline's success. It was discovered at Bell Labs (1947), an open research lab, that had physicists, engineers, electricians, and even pole-climbers researching side by side. After discovery, they licensed the patent for an affordable price (to prevent antitrust complaints on its telephone monopoly) and a rush of companies worked to bring it to market but struggled to mass-produce these tiny switches. 

The US military and NASA contracts didn't care and funded companies like Fairchild Semiconductor to keep going. With the needed resources and incentive at hand, these private companies hired the brightest from the public and private American university system. Scientists soon turned a single transistor into the first integrated circuits (1959) packing several transistors into a single device.

Then the competitive market raced in. Soon transistor radio (late 1950s) and desktop calculator (mid 1960s) sales exploded. With the military, NASA, and now marketplace using transistors, transistor scientists met the capital markets and founded their own companies.

These new semiconductor companies were called "Fairchildren" and they drove development into overdrive. One of them, Intel, invented the microprocessor (1971). Thousands of transistors came on a chip that could be reprogrammed for any application. Development continued according to what became known as "Moore's Law" that said transistor count would double about every two years.

It did. 

Programmable microprocessors now power computers, laptops, smartphones and define the mass-production of diversity. Like Bush envisioned, computers run word processors, dictionaries, web browsers. With transistor counts currently in the tens of billions, computers can do nearly anything we ask. Be it for-profit conglomerates, small companies, or open-source software developed remotely by people around the world.

The point is, in the moderate US, within a few decades the transistor went from undiscovered, to the size of a pencil eraser, to powering home computers and massive super-computers. The West's invention of the transistor was not some ideal world. There was much antagonism. One example, the scientists who started Fairchild Semiconductor were called the "Traitorous Eight" for leaving their employer (a much bigger deal back then), and just barely got funding to start their history changing company. But they, and many others, did. Proving it was not a lucky shot.

The internet, 3D printing, personal computers, GPS, lasers, all had a very similar development cycle. Soviet thinkers like Kitov and Glushkov wrote similar theories about the future of technology. A world where networked computers could process vast amounts of economic information instantly instead of using paper forms and bureaucrats. Both could only write about the future while others built it.

Now, let's take a look at the boardmates needed to birth a new tool. The pipeline includes the obsessive researcher who'd be useless at selling. The government institution-builder who thinks in decades. The risk-taker scientist who quits stable employment. The idealist engineer who gives away expensive software. The dropout who sells ten million computers. The early adopter hacker who keeps tinkering with these new tools.

None would excel at each other's roles. Some do what they do because of genetic traits like intelligence, arrogance, or stubbornness. Others are converts to belief systems of altruism or greed. No ideology covers each person. Each is a different mixture of genotype and phenotype identities. They each have temperaments every society produces and every ecosystem needs to originate new mutations, test which versions are best, produce them, and then predate, scrap, hack, and recycle them to find the energy to keep the process going.

The fight between all these perspectives never ends, and shouldn't. How much public investment is needed, who captures the profit, who carries the risk—that argument is real. But it's a quarrel at the edges like plant life fighting for sunshine or predators for the best hunting grounds. No player should ever envision a place without such competition. From times immemorial, boardmates depend on what other creatures contribute to their shared ecosystem's efficient and continual harvesting of energy to transform useless matter into something useful.

Like in nature, each new creation found a new efficiency, releasing more energy into the ecosystem. The West's moderation meant its people could not only start this process, but keep it going to keep birthing and mass-producing more diverse tools. 

Remember a moderate system knows you can't have a redwood in the desert. Powerful tigers need powerful deer too. Everyone benefits in the ecosystem because a rising tide of adaptations raises all their chances of survival. Thus, strangers or friends, enemies or allies, all their fates are intertwined regardless of the stories anyone believes.

The extremes failed because they selected on the face in the shell, telling half they were special and the rest they weren’t needed.

But something is still missing. If the tool creation pipeline needs all perspectives, somebody still has to decide which tools get built and which get abandoned.

So who decides? It can't be an ideology as they can't pick winners or assign roles. It is someone who is well practiced in selecting the players of the wonderfully moderate ecosystem.

Will AI take your job? You can soon answer that yourself. It’s time to meet the coach in the Game of Technological Life.

## Story—New Tools

The auto claims call-in queue was a grind. Its frickin bell kept us wrapped in endless DING DING DING of stress, frustration, disillusionment. 

Early on I asked my manager if I could go to the washroom. He replied, "Obviously, don't ask me that."

I assumed there was some staffing coordination, but no. Everyone just kept rushing around. So, to reply to emails, voicemails, and make outbound calls, we had to find gaps among the DINGS. 

It was futile. Shit was rolling downhill from head-office, and my manager made sure I knew it. My direct manager was an honest guy, but so stressed he sometimes slept in his car. Within months he went full gray.

I needed to adapt. Why not borrow from my days at the grimy-carpeted software company where I’d worked during university?

So here's what I did.

After each call, adjusters wrote custom emails explaining an insured's next steps. I used Outlook's signature feature to build a few standard email next steps templates. It saved two to five minutes per call. Others copied it.

I also made a tiny app to track phone calls. Instead of being judged only on call volume, I could now show who called, what they wanted, and how I helped. Then I made another app to help write our phone call notes. Not faster, but standardized and easier to update.

I showed my gray-haired, sleep-deprived boss. He showed higher management. And then: "It costs too much to implement." I could use my tools, but they wouldn't be officially adopted. It was all free, by the way.

They never asked how it worked or why I made it. Lower management eventually made their own version of the notes form inside Word. My manager left for a bank.

The demands to answer more calls never stopped.

I could end there and make it sound damning. Insurance management didn't want to speed up their own claims department!

But the problem didn't start with them.

Inventing and adopting new tools is part of our personal lives, but not our education or work culture. There's no curriculum "how to learn new tools". Remember the Milgram experiment, where the teacher shocked the learner instead of handing him a pencil. Authority figures too often want to be the person holding the pencil giving the test as if the other person were a competitor, not a boardmate staffing our shared ecosystem.

Despite so much technomass in our world, there are some laws protecting new creations like patents, but getting and defending them is very expensive. More important, there are none on adoption based on environmental needs, no call for an amendment about protecting predation of slow technologies.

Here's the same blindness elsewhere.

I got a report from another insurance company that said my insured reversed into theirs and is 100% at-fault.

My insured said the other driver's story was bogus. He hadn't been in a crash. He hadn't even been in the city!

I emailed the other adjuster with that, and he replied with a picture I'll never forget.

It was taken by the other driver leaning out his window. It was the sharp photo of a moderately-sized city main street. A white van in front, same make as my insured's. The plates were clean, readable, and a match. Street signs were very legible. The town clock tower was even in the background, with the correct time showing.

And then, my insured's face, visible in the reflection of his side mirror. He was frowning, like a man who has just reversed into somebody.

Images don't show movement, so technically I couldn't use this picture to confirm liability. But I sent it to my insured and asked him to comment. He replied, more or less, "I'm sorry." I imagine he typed it with the same guilty expression I'd just seen in his mirror.

The point is, this photograph was one in a million. Most of the time there's no picture or video, the dispute over what happened drags on for days, and that bell keeps ringing.

But, dash cams are cheap, available, and uncommon. They can lower rates, cut fraud, and settle disputed crashes, all in one little camera. Instead we have pictures of food, animals, rants blaming someone else for endless car traffic, rising insurance rates, and waiting on hold for your insurance adjuster.

Keep note, it's not just _them_ who reject new tools.

## Chapter 14—Meeting the Coach

To us, in everyday life, everything falls downward. It seems the heavier a thing is, the faster it falls, the harder it hits, and the more sweat it takes to move it. In order to walk, run, or jump, the skeletons, muscles, and instincts of squirrels to tigers must adapt to this rule of the game.

When we became self-aware, peoples across the globe had to explain. We used stories, often using the stellar objects as characters in the grand explanation of the cosmos. With these numberless mythologies came the means to know the seasons and how to navigate.

Soon, some people tried to move beyond our perceptions and find the rules that governed our world. Aristotle was among them, and he used philosophy to explain many things, among them why some things fall, others float, and what the stars are. He said things have a natural place, and thus heavier things like soil and rock fall down, while lighter things like fire and air rise up. Earth was thus the center of the cosmos, with lighter things moving around it like leaves caught in the wind.

Aristotle's geocentric astronomy followed. It out-competed the heliocentric system of Aristarchus, which couldn’t explain why, if the Earth moves, the stars never appear to shift. The geocentric Ptolemaic astronomy and star charts guided navigators on land and sea and told farmers the seasons quite well, as long as they were updated every few decades.

Eventually a history of errors piled up and fueled questions. Copernicus found Aristarchus's forgotten heliocentric ideas and updated them with modern mathematics. Galileo used a telescope to see Jupiter's moons and proved not everything moved around the Earth. Tycho Brahe took fantastically accurate stellar records and tried to merge the two astronomies. His assistant, with bad eyesight but excellent math Johannes Kepler did years of calculations and eventually saw something else in the data.

The planets do not move in perfect circles as everyone had always assumed. Each moves in a slight oval. Despite not knowing why, Kepler wrote the laws of planetary motion. Newton took these laws, invented a new math to understand them, and wrote the universal laws of motion. It was here that someone understood that things, from the baby crawling on the ground, to the stars above, move according to the same laws. Newton's math was spectacularly accurate, although the underlying cause of gravity was a mystery.

Enter Einstein. Gravity is not the force we experience in everyday life like hammer and anvil, but a curvature of spacetime. Mass and energy bend it, while objects follow its curves. With Einstein our astronomy is now accurate enough to predict eclipses centuries into the future and keep GPS accurate to within a few feet.

As we meet the coach, this brief history of astronomy shows us something vital. 

All life lives on the game board and therefore is bound by rulebook to find and harvest pockets of useful energy to keep itself organized. Whether by mutation of new genes or the learning of new lessons, the ones who succeed make more copies of themselves, and thus form the species who represent life.

We've already made the point that a species is a combination of genotype, phenotype, and extended phenotype. Ideas are part of our extended phenotype, and like any adaptation, they evolve.

There was no mass understanding of astronomy and gravity. Like the mutation of a dark peppered-moth, each new step was first adopted by a few people. While ideas often need tools like abacuses, telescopes, and computers to develop, ideas evolve like genotype, phenotype, and extended phenotype do.

And that is because of who the coach is. 

Evolution selects individuals. Alone. We can of course help a nephew or defend a stranger, but the main focus is still an individual. Evolution doesn't wait for a group or a movement to form. As renowned biologist Ernst Mayr says, "individual lifeforms are the unit of selection."

Evolution counts one lifeform at a time. That means you and whatever you're holding and whatever you believe. Gravity doesn't act on our cells. A navigator must move her body north or south. The player of the game is an individual. Seeing that clearly is what this book is about. An individual needs energy to stay together, which means it exists in the lifecycle of useful to useless energy.

Whichever individual finds a niche as a scavenger, consumer, predator or other ecosystem role and has kids, is therefore selected to keep doing it.

Biological players don't need to realize evolution's rules. A dog or shark will never understand gravity or use star charts. They don't need to. If a tree produces few nuts, or its nuts are not nutritious, it will be outplanted by the ones who are. Biological life adapts to the rulebook by trial and error. Boardmates that harvest more energy for their ecosystems are naturally selected without ever realizing the dimensions of the world they live in.

We do need to realize this.

Because we have choice, we can practice artificial selection. We can be raccoons taking from an ecosystem and giving little back. The squirrel plants forests by accident. We’re the first player who can plant on purpose.

In the early 1980s Yuri Andropov ran the all-powerful Soviet spy agency KGB and then he ruled the entire country as virtual dictator. When speaking to his leadership comrades he admitted, "we don't know the society in which we live." Decades of falsified statistics and rigid doctrine had blinded the state to its economy and people. The regime with the world's best intelligence apparatus did not understand why its reports said prosperity and progress, while the country's food market shelves were empty, coal miners didn't have soap to clean their faces, and nuclear power stations were blowing up.

Such is the power of human belief systems.

As we've seen, living inside imagined ecosystems is not a Soviet problem. It's a human one, that happens whenever the distance between selecting tools and its consequences gets too far apart. Allied generals had bunkers and battle plans ensuring victory, the same way Soviet leaders were planning an entire economy, the same way kings had grand palaces and spiritual reasons why they were born to rule.

While we've studied physics, chemistry, astronomy, and biological evolution as sciences, we have not studied technological evolution.

Techvolution's new identity won't stop misconceptions, hubris, or fanciful realities. Human groups need hierarchies and beliefs and they will always drift around with human error. But these exist in engineering, mathematics, medicine too and not nearly bad enough to take down entire societies. Heck, String Theory went from possibly explaining the biggest secrets in physics in the 1990s to now increasingly sidelined for its inability to produce a single testable prediction. Everyone knew it might end this way, and it was always okay to say so. Now cutting-edge funding and students drift elsewhere while its defenders are allowed to keep researching because, after all, it took decades for Copernicus's ideas to get the evidence they needed to become obvious.

Physics and the other sciences have this freedom because they measure external forces and value prediction and results above social position and intent. We can measure technological growth, speciation, migration, predators, and grazers because each has a role in the energy flow. The same as in any biological ecosystem.

We already do much of the work. Industrial ecology tracks material and energy flows through economies the way an ecologist tracks them through a forest. Life-cycle assessment measures what a product costs from extraction to disposal. Diffusion research measures one technology displacing another. Insurance assesses failure risks in vehicles, homes, businesses.

What's missing is joining these insights into a unified account of where our food, warmth, and transport actually come from, and the rules of nature that govern their health, growth, divergence, and death. It's like trying to understand the ecology of a forest without knowing about evolution that slowly configured the oak, lotus, bee, raccoon, and squirrel who make it up. 

And, as we near meeting the coach, it's helpful to note developing our understanding ecology, zoology, and biology was quite similar to the history of gravity and astronomy.

It started with mythological stories from peoples around the world. Aristotle looked for natural truths, and grouped animals by visible traits such as blood or no blood, and live young vs. eggs. As explorers brought back many new species from around the world, naturalists began sorting organisms into genera and species like a tree having fleshy fruit or dry seed pods.

Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) formalized this approach as a nested hierarchy (kingdom, class, order, genus, species). Each species received a two-part name, like Tyrannosaurus rex or Homo sapiens. This system worked well, but naturalists still defined a species based on what they could see.

Darwin changed the meaning of those groupings. He showed that species can be related without looking alike. For example, the Galápagos finches have very different beaks, good for eating seeds, insects, or nectar. Yet despite this different appearance, they descend from the same finches who landed on the remote islands. Classification by common descent organized the myriad species into a coherent tree that's largely held up to genetic testing. How else would we explain why hippos and whales have similar DNA?

As in physics and astronomy, each of these ideas was adopted because it explained the natural world better than the last. Like with gravity and the planets, the species themselves never changed being who they were. Our labels of them slowly became more of their natural identities. Each new understanding got closer to nature’s answer because it was a universal understanding of gravity and evolution that did not depend on the story of the person telling it.

This same process applies to us, here and now. Nature doesn’t hand out name-tags. We know iron by its 26 protons and its melting point, a tiger by its stripes, its niche, and how it finds energy. Nature can’t read our name-tags either. Friends see Matthew, Sarah, Apple, Google. Economics sees pilot, bureaucrat, mechanic, welder. Politics sees left, right, rich, donor. Nature sees predator, plant-eater, decomposer, scavenger.

Like the iron atom, we have a number: the energy we need to keep warm, raise our young, and survive another day. Whatever anyone thinks or believes, that number has to be paid. It’s the one name-tag we can’t take off.

Our new identity is being a self-aware selector inside a technological ecosystem: someone who knows every person carries that number, understands others by it, and works with them to pay it more efficiently, freeing energy for the rest of life. That is the study of techvolution.

Nature still keeps the score; the scoreboard never lies. But the coach, the one choosing which tools get played, is you. It always was, because we replaced nature’s coach with ourselves long ago, every time we bought one tool over another, voted for one rule over another, or kept a habit instead of changing it.

Choosing to compost your daily banana keeps matter useful. Opting for the stairs trains your cardio to hold off entropy a little longer. Repairing an old appliance saves much material and energy. Calling an old friend rekindles a fading friendship. Having pictures of your damaged car makes a claim go much faster.

Just as squirrels and raccoons have an ecological identity, you already have a technological one. Like gravity, it was always there. It becomes new when you adopt it. 

You’ve already met a team of coaches. Remember Maria’s customers. No law shut down her DVD stores, and no one voted on it. Millions of ordinary people, one evening at a time, chose to stay home and press play instead of driving to the store. Each choice was small. Together, they selected the DVD out of the game, and selected streaming in. That is what coaching looks like: not a speech, not a belief, just a choice of tool, repeated until the board changes.

None of this is a revelation. Storms and illness wearing things down, kids and new tools being how we keep score: you knew every piece already. We just never assembled them into the Game of Life, the same way people watched things fall for thousands of years before anyone called it gravity.

So will AI take your job?

Ask the coach. That means you, and the people your work serves. Not the boss with the title, but the customer who calls you for help.

Don’t wait for a pundit’s prediction. You don’t need a prediction anymore than you need a promise from a doctor, mechanic, or claims adjuster; you need the number. Take the tasks your job is made of and try them with AI. Where it does a task faster and cheaper, that task will go, the way the drive to the video store went. That isn’t the end of you. It’s energy freed up. The label on your job may expire, but the role underneath it, getting food, warmth, transport, or stories to people, doesn’t. And where AI can’t do the task, you’ve found the part of your job the board still needs from you. The only people truly threatened are the ones defending a label. Our understanding of gravity, the stars, and life itself kept improving because people let old labels go.

Remember the Prologue: your paycheck is a species, and nobody had told you which one yours is. Now you can find out yourself. Look past its label to where it sits in the energy flow.

### Giving Tree

Light is high. The foraging party left nine days ago. Far from our home. Far from the river.

I try. The stone scraper is sharp. Bits of deer flesh come off.

"Good, Kai. Now here. This part has fat. Remove."

I scrape where she points. My arms hurt but I keep working. I hear whimpering. A puppy wants the fat I'm cleaning. I throw some fat to it.

### The Power of Signs

The taro plants need water. I carry the gourd from the stream, pouring carefully around each root as my mother taught me. I pass friends making baskets, feeding pigs, collecting water. This land feeds us because we work it together.

By the time I finish, the sun is setting. I check my tools—plow, shovel, axe—making sure each is clean.

I go watch what they are doing down at the beach. Twelve long boats with huge logs lashed to either side. The shipbuilders have been building for two seasons.

As the sun sets, an Elder sits beside me.

"How will they get there?" I ask.

"You should say, 'how will we get there.' They, I, are alone and go nowhere...We can go anywhere."

The Elder looks up at the night sky. "What do you see?"

"The gods."

"Can you read the gods' signs?"

"I know when the rains will come."

"As you should. You read Rongo signs to know which movements mean the waters will fall. But there is more for us to know, remember, pass on. How did we get here to plant energy rich food right before the rains come?"

I look blankly.

The Elder stares at me, smiles, says with slow speech, "You always must believe in the signs. In the time of the seventh Navigator, when the fish were too few for our ancient people, the Chief asked the priests to read the signs. They read the signs of Tāne, who showed birds flying beyond the horizon to show when land waits beyond sight.

The gods were calling us to voyage.

We sent our scouts. In small ships they track the birds far into the ocean. Navigators reading the stars to map our voyage as far as they could. 

Meanwhile, we built twelve canoes according to the patterns the gods revealed in the first days. Each held twenty of us. Some planters who, like you, read Rongo's signs, fishers who honored Tangaroa's tapu, builders who understood Tāne's gifts of hard and soft woods.

The Navigator carried the red arrow his father's father's father carved. Seven white shells bound to its shaft—one for each Navigator in his line, each voyage that brought us here, island by island. Seven generations. Seven new homes found.

We paddled into the endless ocean. For sixteen days we saw nothing but water and sky. Ocean. Sun. Waves. Ocean. Sun. Waves. Heat. Thirst. Hunger.

Ocean. Sun. Waves. Thirst. Hunger.

The Navigator read the gods' signs each night—stars, currents, wind from the north.

On the seventeenth morning, we saw land.

Weak, tired, dazed we leapt from the boats onto the sand.

The Fire-Keeper pulled herself onto the beach, ready to start the fire that would give us fresh water to drink.

People were already there.

Our warriors held their spears, waiting for the battle.

We had no chance. No gods can save the dead.

We chanted our lineage. An old woman answered, naming ancestors we knew. We had met cousins.

We made peace.

We showed them our black stone blades, sharper than theirs. "From the mountain island," we said. The old woman's people leaned close to see. "We gave fish for stone, shells for taro. Each island holds what the others do not. We told them where our home island was. Gave them our families' names. When we trade, no one goes hungry."

Cousins became friends. The island was small. Too small for us to stay. We stayed enough to get strong again.

Then we moved on. Found a new island.

Built shelters, planted the taro, fished the waters. We raised the red arrow on new stones, honoring the gods who carried us. Now we trade with all islands. Now we're always strong.

"Because we believed in the gods' signs." he says. "You plant our food here. Someday, you may follow the birds and lights and go out to plant new lands in new places. The gods give us the signs. ### The Limits of Signs

The merchant ship rounds the headland at dawn. I've sailed from Phasis, Sinope, and every port in between. My hold is full of wares—Eastern textiles, Scythian gold, carved ivory from far beyond. Three months of favorable winds. Poseidon has been kind.

But as we approach the Lindos harbor, I see something is wrong.

Half the town is gone. Where warehouses stood, there is only rubble. Fishing boats lie shattered far up the beach. People move through debris, placing scraps of wood and stone on carts.

We anchor offshore. I row in with my first mate.

We walk the shore together. Rubble, ruins, rotten foodstuffs are everywhere.

But we are Greeks. We work. We build. We are moving already. I am proud.

I see a compatriot and his family. They search the rubble. Sort it into piles. Making use of it. He is happy.

Another Greek can't help but comment on the sight, "We call him Raven. He keeps everything he touches in the hills. Now that we have nothing, he will make a bundle."

"Good for him." I reply.

We walk on. An old woman sits beside temple ruins, sobbing.

"What happened to your city?" I ask.

"Poseidon," she says. "The earth-shaker's wrath."

I don't correct her. I've seen three earthquakes in twenty years of sailing—Crete, Kos, now Lindos. Always near islands. Always near deep water. Now two followed by waves. I don't know why. But I know where to avoid anchoring in winter.

A fisherman approaches, hands bleeding. "Twenty-eight days ago. The ground shook. Then the water pulled back, past the outer rocks. Then the god sent his wave. Tall as three men. Forty homes gone. Seventy-three dead."

I look at the destruction. My cargo is worthless here—no warehouses, no merchants. The trade network is broken. Maybe I will speak with the Raven and barter for something.

"Why was Poseidon angry?"

"The oracle at Delphi has been consulted."

By midday, a priest arrives. The survivors gather in what remains of the agora.

"I have consulted the signs," the priest announces. "Poseidon was angered by insufficient devotion. We built him a temple of modest stone. We offered three bulls. This was not enough."

An old man steps forward. "We've given what we could—"

"The god demands more," the priest interrupts. "When Odysseus angered Poseidon, the god hounded him for ten years. When the Phaeacians helped him, Poseidon turned their ship to stone." He raises his voice. "We will rebuild the temple twice as large. We will sacrifice six bulls at each festival. We will carve Poseidon into marble."

Murmurs of agreement. A woman weeps with relief—finally, an answer.

Then a voice from the back: "Or perhaps we might observe something else."

The crowd turns. A man in a simple tunic steps forward—lean, weathered, perhaps fifty.

"Thales of Miletus," someone whispers.

The priest stiffens. "You dare question the divine signs?"

"I would never question the gods' power. I only observe: Every time a wave like this comes, the earth shakes first. Then the water pulls back. Then the wave. In Miletus, in Phocaea, here today. Always the same pattern."

"Yes," the priest says sharply. "The earth-shaker announces his wrath through the land before he strikes with water."

"Perhaps. But I learned from Babylonian star-watchers when the sun would go dark. It did. Not because I angered Apollo, but because I understood the pattern. The gods may will it—but the pattern itself can be known."

The crowd murmurs, uncertain.

"Build the temple. Honor the gods. I look forward to the festivals." Thales turns toward the hill. "But if anyone wishes to discuss what patterns we might observe, I'll be up there on the hill, looking over the sea that took your city."

I trade with the Raven, an Illyrian named Bato. A knife, shovels, and cloaks for gold he's found.

I shake his hand hard. "Rebuild with us. We are Greeks."

I leave twenty wine flasks and twenty cases of grain to feed the workers. With powerful bulls, rich food, and hard work, energy Greeks will move and rebuild this city. Trade will return. I will move cargo port to port, and return to help civilization regrow here.

I look back at the hill.

Three people look over the sea, discussing what the see with Thales, the philosopher.

### Use of Reason

The city official arrived at dawn with his apprentice and surveyor's tools. They were taking my field for the new raised water channel.

The official unrolled his map. "Your field follows the creek bed, curves with the road. Irregular boundary. One thousand eighty square paces. Your compensation plot is a rectangle—sixty by eighteen. Also one thousand eighty. You'll receive equivalent land near the eastern gate."

I stared at the winding line, then the neat rectangle. "How can you know the area when my boundary twists like that?"

The official looked up, annoyed. "Because I've calculated it."

"But how do I—"

"Because God requires honest dealings. The measurement is written here. You'll receive the deed tomorrow with compensation for two years of lost crops." He rolled the map and walked toward the road, calling back to his apprentice to mark the corners.

The apprentice drove stakes along my field's edge, measuring the base for the raised water channel's pillars. I watched him work, on my father's land. My grandfather's land.

I asked the young apprentice when the official was out of earshot. "I know the surveyor is a faithful man. But I can't read the mathematics. My young son cannot either. Can you help?" 

"Yes. I can help you."

The apprentice knelt and drew in the dirt. He sketched my field's irregular shape, then drew lines inside it, many triangles and rectangles.

"Break it into simple shapes," he said. "This triangle—base times height, then halved. This rectangle—length times width. Add the pieces."

He wrote numbers beside each shape, then summed them. "One thousand eighty."

Then he drew the compensation plot. A clean rectangle. "Sixty times eighteen. Also one thousand eighty."

"Always the same?"

"The mathematics requires it. The shapes look different, but the area is identical."

I looked at the shapes in the dirt. At the irregular boundary broken into pieces.

"Can I measure the new land? Before I accept?"

"Yes," the apprentice said. "The method works. You can verify it yourself."

My son and I measured the new plot with a marked rope. We paced it twice.

Sixty by eighteen.

My son and I made a plan. Prepare the soil. Start planting new vines on the south, trees to the north, bee hives in between. We'll be back to grain crop in one season, tree crops in three to four seasons.

"Faster," my son said.

"How?"

"No more droughts. We'll have constant sunshine and water. Constant growth," my son's points make us both smile.

We took the deed the next day.

That year, the raised water channel brought water from the mountains. The road beside it carried carts between the harbor and city center. Workers laid the foundation for a new storehouse, each corner measured square, each wall calculated to bear its load. We planted and sold our crop.

My farm continued and a new city rose.

### Averroes - Height of Reason

Water flows through graded channels from the mountains, under hilltops and over valleys. It feeds the acequia—a web of channels that carries the mountain water across the dry country—spreads to the horizon, feeding vast orchards.

Men walk the channels, clearing silt, checking the fall of the water to ensure constant flow.

In a nearby courtyard, two students bend over their paper sheets, each drawing a different triangle. A third student holds an astrolabe to the afternoon sky—a disk of engraved brass, the heavens mapped in metal. 

The third student turns the astrolabe, sets it down, and frowns.

"Teacher, the *acequia*—how did the builders chisel the gradient so precisely? The water flow is constant."

"The same geometry you learn now." Averroes taps the paper. "Water falls at the same rate here as in Baghdad, as in Rome before us. If you can do the math."

"But those are Greeks and Persians," another says. "We're not them."

"Mathematics needs no tongue." Averroes is quiet a moment. "What Euclid proved in Alexandria, Al-Khwarizmi made into algebra. What we calculate today, others will use tomorrow. Because a man dies. A library burns. If the mathematics survives, it stays alive for the next mind."

He takes the astrolabe from the third student, holds it to the light.

"But mathematics is not for lessons. A captain a thousand miles from any coast he has seen, takes a star's height with this brass, opens his tables, and knows where he stands on an ocean. So he can see in the darkness." He sets it down.

"We learned all this, about a sky we cannot touch, using the reason and mind God gifted us."

The first student lifts a worn star table from the bench—Al-Battani's, copied and recopied, the ink cracked at the folds. "Then why does my father keep two star tables, Teacher?"

"Because the sky does not hold still for us." Averroes does not look away from him. "The acequia silts up too. We clear it every morning. Stop clearing it a single season and the orchards are desert again. In the same way, the table is not the heavens. It is our best account of them, and an account must be kept—corrected, recopied, carried forward—or it rots like anything left untended. This is the work we do."

The boy asks "The table says the planet should sit here." He points. "But it sits there, a full hand difference."

Averroes looks. He does not flinch from it.

"The Philosopher Aristotle taught us the planets move in perfect circles. Ptolemy taught us to set the planet's center off to one side, away from the earth, and let it speed up and slow as it needed. When we re-calculate, the numbers come back true, and sea captains find their harbors at night."

"The water moves the same, always, everywhere. Why don't the planets?"

Averroes is quiet a long moment.

"I have turned that over for thirty years," he says at last. "The Philosopher Aristotle tells me what the heavens *are*: perfect, turning in true circles, for nothing less would befit them. Ptolemy tells me what they *do*—near enough to cross an ocean by. We do not yet know. So we keep working. Perhaps you will find the reason, my bright student."

The boy smiles.

"Add the correction to the table," Averroes says. "Date it. Send copies west with the next caravan, and some east. Whatever the spheres truly are, farmers must know when to plant. Harbors must keep being found."

The third student writes the new number beside the old one, and updates the star table inside several texts.

He puts one updated text on the library shelf. The others go out by horses and ships to libraries throughout the world.

### Renaissance - Limits of Reason

The woodcutter marks an oak tree. "Can I have this big one?"

"No," Niccolo says. "The four smaller ones beyond it."

They walk the Tuscan tree line and discuss which trees to sell, what price, when to cut them.

The woodcutter pauses. "My son asks why we Italians are so weak now. The Spanish take what they want. The French march through whenever they please. The Germans threatened us without fear. His grandfather said Italians were once masters of the world."

Niccolo stops walking. "Your grandfather was right. Rome ruled for centuries. Do you know how?"

The woodcutter shrugs.

Niccolo picks up a stick, scratches a line in the dirt. "Back then, the Germans lived in huts and walked barefoot, used hollow logs to cross rivers and raid our rich cities. So, Caesar threw a bridge across the Rhine in ten days. Marched his legions over to show the Germans what Rome could build. They thought our hammers were sorcery. They left Rome alone for generations. Over a bridge...Now they raid us with the tools we gave them."

"My wood order is for a bridge at Certaldo." The woodcutter almost smiles. "Smaller than the Rhine, I'd think."

Niccolo gestures down the valley. "Rome held the world together with roads, bridges, walls, the law that ran along them. All of it kept up. Repaired. Paid for in iron and work and virtue, year after year."

"And now?"

"The Spanish armies march on Roman roads. The French winter in our cities. We kept the stones and lost the men who knew how to lay and defend them." He drops the stick. "We read Aristotle and argue about virtue while foreigners hold the gates. We have the ancients' words. Not their work knowledge."

"So what do we need?"

"New modes and orders. Not what the philosophers say *should* work. Imagined thoughts of the world. What does. The effectual truth of the thing—not the dream of it."

The woodcutter nods slowly. Then: "Weren't you exiled? Tortured?"

Niccolo's shoulders drop. "Different times."

"My cousin said six drops on the rope."

"*Four.* And I was innocent." His voice flattens. "I didn't plot against the Medici. I love Florence."

"But you wrote it down. Books."

"Everything I know."

"People read them?"

A pause. "A few."

"Your sons?"

"Not interested in my remedies." He says it evenly. "I write at night."

The woodcutter hefts his axe. "All these printed books now. My cousin says there are more in Florence than a man could read in three lifetimes. What's the point of more words? Let the stars be stars. Let the princes be princes." He turns toward the big oak again. "Can I have that one? It's not sacred, is it?"

"No." Niccolo watches him go. "I'll give it to you."

The woodcutter walks to the tree.

Niccolo stays where he is, talking half to the man's back. "Being at the bottom makes it easier to see the top. I've served princes. Been broken by them. Read the ancients until they answered." The axe rises.

"Don't worry. This will make a good new Roman bridge."

The blade strikes down into the strong Italian oak tree. Over and over.

The tree is weakening. Niccolo says, "Bookshelves," then smiles as the falling tree crashes to the soil. "To wise up we'll need many new books."

### The Method

January wind rattles against the shutters of a small farmhouse. 

Inside three candles burn on a wood table. The months of a calendar are tacked on the wall. The days of November and December are crossed off with a spattering of "No result" on Friday and Saturday.

A boy carries a box of metal rods and small metal boxes with holes punched in the lids and a slit along the side.

"Thanks, William," says the merchant as he places the candles inside the metal boxes. "Watch William. The slit is for these new rods. With wax on the far end, we attach the metal into the flame."

"We've made changes. We're using the same candle. But with different air sources. This one gets air from outside. This one draws from inside the chimney, above the fire. This one just gets the room air, here by the hearth."

The blacksmith nods. "He helped me make the new contraptions."

William now adds, "We're testing that there's something *in* the air that makes fire burn differently."

Merchant jumps in, "But it's a guess. Because we don't know what's in air."

William responds, "What else would we do? We're testing different environmental conditions. To measure which burns hottest."

The merchant, "There's lots of other ways. More profitable ones too."

William, "Like what?"

The blacksmith looks at his apprentice, "By doing what works without knowing why. How do we measure hottest?"

"Tip resting on wax at the far end. Whichever wax melts first is the hottest flame. And therefore has the strongest fuel."

The merchant and blacksmith smile at the apprentice.

Each lights a candle and places their boxed candle at its location.

The merchant flips the sand clock.

"The chimney candle is dying. Rod wax not melting. Snuffed at 1/10 of glass." The blacksmith writes it down.

"The room-air rod is dripping wax. 2/10 of glass." The merchant glances over. "William, your status?"

"Window candle burning. Wax melting now—4/10 of glass."

"Something in chimney air *kills* fire. We already knew that," the blacksmith declares.

"Or something *isn't* in the air to fuel flame. But look—the room candle burned hotter than the window one. Both have fresh air. Why the difference?"

The merchant stops, pen above paper. "Fire eats something out of the air. That's one finding. But there's another. The room air was warmer. The window air comes in cold off the street. Warm air feeds the flame better than cold."

"Two findings at once."

"Two. We have to test them apart. Next time we warm the window air in a pan before it reaches the candle. See what happens."

*Test 5, January. Chimney air—candle dies at 1/10. Room air (warm) melts wax at 2/10. Window air (cold) at 4/10.*

*Supposition: fire consumes something from air. Spent air will not support flame.*

*Second supposition: cold air weakens the flame even when fresh. To separate the two, warm the window air before it reaches the candle.*

"Maybe it's us?" says the apprentice.

"Us?"

"I was looking close. I breathed on the flame. It got dimmer—like the chimney one."

The blacksmith smiles at his apprentice. The merchant sets down his pen.

"Lord Bacon would have liked that. The boy notices what we missed. My master would have slapped me for saying it."

"Add breath to the list of variables," the merchant says. "Air from a room with many people breathing."

"Now we must test more?" says William.

"We get to test more."

The blacksmith picks up the book lying nearby. "Novum Organum! True directions concerning the interpretation of nature! We are not in the old world of the Mediterranean filled with superstitions."

The merchant adds, "William, Bacon wrote that we command nature by obeying her. But that work is too great for one lifetime. Like St. Paul's Cathedral, everyone lays bricks others build on."

"By testing," says the blacksmith.

"And keeping records," adds the merchant.

"Why?" asks the apprentice.

"So anyone can redo our work. If they cannot reproduce our results, we have proved nothing. This project will take a long time. It's worth doing, William."

"I still don't understand how else we could discover what fuels fire?"

The blacksmith jumps in, "Mankind has always wondered. Now we have the tools of discovery. So we have the responsibility to find out! Imagine the metals we can make! Imagine what the new metals can fashion. Don't look back! Imagine William! Imagine! That is for you and your apprentice after you to build on. Are you ready?"

The apprentice smiles.

They relight the chimney candle one more time, each placing new iron rods into new flames.

A bookshelf fills with notepads of observations. 

### Extinction Event

I enter the House of Commons with soldiers behind me.

This should not be necessary.

Father never marched armed men into Parliament. It is 1642. My ancestors kept order inside our realm without soldiers. But the Five Members—Pym, Hampden, Haselrig, Holles, Strode—these Puritans refused to send my money to their king. Stirred rebellion. They turned the realm against its own king.  

I only want the traitorous five.

I look around the Parliament. It is too quiet. No one is acknowledging their king.

I approach the Speaker of the House. "Mr. Speaker, do you see any of these persons in the House?"

He kneels. Won't look at me. "May it please Your Majesty, I have neither eyes to see nor tongue to speak in this place but as the House is pleased to direct me."

He disobeys? I am their king.

"No matter, I think my eyes are as good as another's."

I see the benches. Front, high, back. Members stare back, silent. No one points. No one speaks. No one helps their king find traitors in their midst. I am confused. Frustrated. I can't let them see it.

The Five seats are empty. Someone warned them. Someone here, watching me now with blank faces.

It is unjust.

"I see all my birds have flown."

I should speak more. Command them to produce the traitors.

My throat tightens. The words catch.

No. A king does not explain himself to subjects. The moment I justify myself I have already surrendered what they are trying to take. Kings cannot be judged by subjects. I am the Sovereign.

But they want the money. The army. The bridges, the buildings, the land. These artisans, craftsmen, merchants—this middling sort—they want the realm. They hold the coin and will not send it any longer, and a king without coin is a realm in chaos.

I will leave with dignity. The soldiers make way. I exit the House of Commons. 

"Privilege! Members of the Commons are protected by Privilege!"

Unholy rabble. Ignore them for now.

Outside, on London streets, the middling crowd watch from doorways and corners. Not cheering. Not bowing. Watching.

My coach has stopped far away. The driver is on the ground, the rear wheel jammed against a curb. He pulls at it. A wheel is broken.

My retinue gathers. The men lift the coach, work on the wheel. The crowd's attention turns to me. I see them through the bodies of the soldiers.

They watch me. I must do something.

I walk to the coach. The driver looks up. I see the parts in his hands—the spoke, the hub, the iron rim. I do not know which goes where. I am a king.

The people are watching. Parliament is watching.

"Broken things happen. Keep going. Well done. Well done."

The wheel is changed. The crowd cheers. I wait to depart. Did I see the driver smile at the cheering?

As I enter the coach, "Send word for the alderman to meet us."

Soldiers bow, "Your Majesty", then depart on horse.

The King's Street is full. The people are many. We shall seek the surrender of the traitorous five at the Guildhall.

The coach moves slowly. I watch the city go by me.

I see the realm. Our loyal subjects. Workers laying roads. Bakers making bread. Clothiers, smiths, shipwrights, masons—a whole realm making itself.

I have not seen most of it. I do not understand it. And yet it builds through our order. I protect men of knowledge. I fund their research. The people visit our magnificent churches to show their weekly devotion.

Together our ancestors turned wood forts into stone castles, castles into palaces. Kingly protection made the roads and farms and canals safe from Scots and French and rebellious lords. A strong monarch kept the realm from tearing itself apart.

We arrive at the Guildhall. The traitors are inside. This troublesome episode is near its end.

I step down. I look for the aldermen who enforce order in the City, they must be waiting to receive their King. The crowd is large. Somewhere close are my little birds.

"Privilege! Privilege! Privilege!" is chanted from our subjects. The traitors have fled and left their agents to cause agitation.

"Shall we return to Whitehall Palace, Your Majesty?"

I nod. What else can I do? 

My coach and guard are going home. We must return to the King's Street.

The driver turns down a strange lane. I do not know these streets. Where am I? I cannot return home from here.

He turns again. A new unknown road in a foreign part of town. 

Is this servant with Parliament? Are our loyal soldiers nearby?

I shall not show my fear. A king can be killed, but kingship cannot. I look at the people's faces. They know everything I have done, that our ancestors did. Everything I defended, they defended. If I am guilty, so is the realm.

That cannot be true.

What do these Puritans and Presbyterians want. To challenge the Crown? To what end? Do they want to practice religion freely? They want Sovereignty.

I am their king. Chosen by God, given the duties of God. It is in the Scriptures, laws, and customs. The faithful people know it.

The coach stops at Whitehall Palace. This is good. I am hungry. Thirsty. Scared. Cold. 

Be kingly. I cannot let our subjects see feelings. My coach door opens. A hand steadies me down. "Your Majesty."

The magnificent gates open. The dozens of guards bow.

I am home. Walking through wonder and faith. Hundreds of rooms. Marble ceilings. Paintings by the world's finest artists. Daily work for carpenters, masons, turners, coopers, glaziers, joiners, blacksmiths, tailors, weavers, cooks, bakers, gardeners, grooms, chandlers. A host of workers, servants, and orderlies with families housed and fed.

Perhaps we should allow the middling sort to walk the palace too. So they can see the Realm's magnificence and my generosity.

Perhaps. For now the courtroom doors open ahead of me.

"Your Majesty."
"Your Majesty."

I enter the dining hall. Grand as a cathedral, dozens of nobles await.

The fire is lit. The table is set, the drink is ready, the meat is carved. I look forward to having a meal.

I am to be announced.

"Charles, by the Grace of God, King of England, Scotland, France and Ireland, Defender of the Faith."

Music plays.

Every lord bows. Ladies curtsey. Good.

"Your Majesty, I am your most humble servant."
"Your Majesty, I am your most humble servant."
"Your Majesty, I am your most humble servant."

"Let us pray"

"Lighten our darkness, we beseech thee O Lord; we pray: and by thy great mercy defend us from all perils and dangers of this night; for the love of our Saviour. Amen"

I am to speak.

The room waits.

Knights look to Barons, who look to Earls, who look to Dukes. Everyone waits for their King's words.

I say, "Our loyal subjects defy their King. By God's grace the ancient rights of the Crown, and the laws and customs of this realm will be enforced. The north is free. The farmers and guilds are devout. The faith of the nobles will secure the realm."

My words do not catch, such is God's Divine message.

The room erupts.

"Long live the King!"

"For God and King Charles!"

"For the Crown and the Church of England!"

"The Majesty's two bodies shall be preserved."

An Earl rises, hand to heart. "A bold and glorious course, Majesty. I am wholly with the Crown." All praise the wisdom.

No one asks where the coin is. No one asks how many men-at-arms need muster. The order does not need such questions. The order only needs to be passed down. Some are flatterers. Conflict will reveal them. I must be aware. They pledge their love in the longest words and risk nothing. Most are loyal to God.

I say "We will summon the militia. These two Englands will become one realm, once more. Our ancestors used sword and spear. We must use gun and cannon."

Now we do as our ancestors did and prepare for war.

We discover which noble has fought before. Some have read about Europe's ongoing wars. These will advise the commands of the highest born. We toast the victories to come, and our impending return to London.

Earl Johnson hushes the crowd's excitement. He has received a letter: "Most gracious Sovereign. Call the Banners. We shall rally at Nottingham. Your Majesty's most humble and obedient servant, Prince Rupert!"

The Prince's courage in fighting with Houses Vasa and Hohenzollern against House Habsburg in Europe gives him great renown. A burst of activity follows. Each lord speaks louder than the last, vows grander than the last. The nobles give a great many commands and dictate many orders and letters.

Servants do the rest. Good. The system is working.

Among the celebrations, I look up at the marvelous painting of my father. Done by the greatest artists in Europe. I see my father, King James, ascend into Heaven, gold and silver lines showing the justice of our Divine Right. The middling sort count coin and ledgers. We count centuries. Art. Glory. Faith. Loyalty. Inheritance. Position.

I depart now for war. But I will return, father. I will do our noble house justice. I will save the realm. I will save our people.

I am their king.

The king raises his standard at Nottingham. Most lords ride to him and their tenant farmers follow. Some others join the merchants and craftsmen of the south and build their own army, centered on London. The country is at civil war; Royalist versus Parliamentarians. Both sides have the same food, equipment, weapons. One side organises by lordship, bloodline, and obligation. The other through independence, merit, and separation of political and military leadership.

Battles take place at Edgehill, Marston Moor, Naseby, and more.

Sixty major engagements. 84,000 soldiers dead. Many more wounded. Four years of war. Massive disorder everywhere. Many fields untilled. Homes abandoned. Roads unrepaired. Villages burned for sheltering the wrong army. Wilderness creeps back into civilization. Over a hundred thousand non-combatants die from starvation, infections, blight. 

Parliament wins. Charles in prison.

He secretly raises a new army. Now Scottish lords call their tenants from the field. Farmers follow. Ploughs left in furrows. Fields go to weed. Granaries dwindle. Charles asks Irish Catholics to help defend his Protestant throne causing countrywide revulsion. 

Parliament's support swells. Their battle-hardened New Model Army readies for another war.

More battles. More dead. Same result. The king defeated.

Parliament infuriated. The king (everyone still calls him that) put on trial. 135 commissioners appointed. Half refuse to attend. The angry half in control.

When asked, Charles refuses to plead innocent or guilty. The stammer that has caught his words all his life, is gone. He speaks aloud, clear and certain—not to the judges, himself, his father, or God, but to the people. "No earthly power can justly call me in question." He is right, by every law that has ever existed, at least since the Roman legions left the land.

Charles declared guilty for "high treason, waging war against the realm, and betraying the trust of the people".

The king held prisoner at his old home, Whitehall Palace, London. Once more, he looks upon his father in the magnificent mural painting, being taken to Heaven by God's angels...while a scaffold and chopping block are built outside.

The people assemble to watch.

Last words. "As for the People, I desire their liberty and freedom, as much as any whosoever; but I must tell you...A Subject and a Sovereign are clean different things."

Last word: "Remember".

Axe raised. One clean stroke. A king is dead.

A republic is declared.

Parliament rules. Eleven years of Puritan government—no theatre, no Christmas, no dancing. Churches stripped bare. Stained glass smashed, statues broken. Royal banners torn down.

The army argues with Parliament. Parliament argues with itself. Nobody agrees on anything except that this isn't working.

The people remember. England is a realm, and a realm needs a king.

Charles's son is declared king. Charles II rides into London. The crowds cheer. The banners go back up. The theatres reopen. Taverns swell. The King's murderers are hunted. The worst are executed. England goes back to work.

Charles II leans toward the foreign faith, Catholicism, in a devoutly Protestant England. His brother James succeeds him, and wears that old faith openly.

Parliament is furious. The people are scared.

New Protestant monarchs, William and Mary from Holland, are installed—and paid by Parliament—in a Glorious Revolution. England now stays at work.

Before, during, and after the bitter wars of theology in England and Continental Europe, members from all faiths wanted out of the carnage.

New soil. No king.

Massachusetts. Connecticut. Rhode Island. Congregations governing themselves. Towns voting on their own affairs. A quiet experiment in self-rule seeds itself, growing in the cold northern soil.

*"Separation of military, executive, legislative, judicial powers."*
*"Separation of church and state."*
*"Freedom of religion."*
*"All men are created equal..."*

Not universal. Not quite. Not yet.

But the seed is growing.

### Universal Laws of Nature

**From Edmond Halley, London**  
**To Christopher Wren, Royal Society**  
**14 March 1705**

Wren,

I have been studying comet observations spanning two centuries. Isaac Newton's mathematics may reveal something extraordinary.

Three comets appeared in 1531, 1607, and 1682. I observed the last one myself in September. The orbital elements—the path, the speed, the angle—are nearly identical for all three.

What if they are not three comets, but one comet returning?

The interval: 76 years between each appearance.

If Newton's law of universal gravitation governs comets as it does planets, this comet should return in 1758.

Mathematics included for your review.

—Halley

**From Christopher Wren, Royal Society**  
**To David Gregory, Oxford**  
**22 March 1705**

Gregory,

Halley claims he has identified a periodic comet using Isaac Newton's gravitational laws. He predicts its return in 1758—53 years hence.

Can you verify his orbital calculations? If he is correct, this would be the first prediction of a comet's return in history. Newton's mathematics would govern not just planets and moons, but comets as well.

One law for all celestial bodies.

Mathematics included for your review.

—Wren

**From David Gregory, Oxford**  
**To Christopher Wren, Royal Society**  
**5 April 1705**

Wren,

I have checked Halley's calculations against the observations from 1531, 1607, and 1682. The orbital elements match within the margin of observational error.

The giant planets, Jupiter and Saturn, may pull the comet slightly off its course as it passes—enough to delay its return. By how much, I cannot yet say. The calculation is beyond what any of us can finish at this time.

Halley's method is sound. If the comet returns near 1758, it will prove Newton's law governs the entire heavens—planets, moons, and comets alike.

I pray one of us will live to see it.

—Gregory

**From Edmond Halley, London**  
**To Christopher Wren, Royal Society**  
**18 April 1705**

Wren,

I am publishing my findings in the *Synopsis of the Astronomy of Comets*. Gregory's confirmation strengthens the case.

The comet will return in 1758. The mathematics knows this before any eye can see it. 

What might we accomplish with such knowledge? If we can predict a comet's return 53 years hence, what else might Sir Isaac Newton's laws reveal?

Future astronomers will test this prediction and prove us right or wrong.

I do wish I could live to see it. But I do believe it will happen.

—Halley

The comet has returned, exactly as Halley predicted.

I observed it tonight, Christmas night, through my telescope. The orbital path matches the calculations made 53 years ago.

The mathematics was right.

Newton's law of universal gravitation governs the heavens. One law for planets, moons, and comets alike.

Halley did not live to see this night. But his mathematics did.

—Palitzsch

### Universal Laws for Life

The fossil shop stands between a bookseller and a tinsmith on Lyme Regis's main street. Two men pause outside, examining the specimens in the window, then enter.

Mary Anning looks up from her workbench. "Looking for something in particular?"

"The most interesting you have," Mr. Hartley says.

Mary sets three specimens on the counter.

"These are from different soil layers," she says. "This coiled shell, ridged and chambered is from thirty feet down. This large vertebra from sixty feet. And this serrated, curved tooth from ninety feet."

Mr. Hartley leans closer. "Different animals?"

"Different worlds," Mary says.

Mr. Webb frowns. "Worlds?"

"We dig to reach Roman ruins. These are deeper still. What lived at ninety feet never lived at thirty feet. What lived at thirty feet is gone now."

"Gone," Mr. Hartley says. "Extinct?"

"Yes."

Mr. Hartley studies the vertebra. "So these died long before humans."

"Long before," Mary says.

The two men look at each other.

"How long?" Mr. Webb asks.

"I don't know. Longer than we have words for."

Mr. Hartley points to the coiled shell. "And nothing like this lives now?"

"Nothing. I've looked. So have others."

Mr. Webb exhales slowly. "So you say God's creation...can end?"

Mary doesn't answer.

"Perhaps, we have yet to find your lost creatures in the unexplored areas of our world."

Miss Anning doesn't respond. Mr. Hartley says, "How much, Miss Anning?"

"Two shillings for the ammonite. Five for the vertebra. Three for the tooth."

"We'll take all three."

Mary wraps them in cloth, takes their coins.

"We are members of the Kent Naturalists Society. Rock fossils have stirred up much debate in our group. May we join you on your next excavation?" Mr. Hartley asks.

Mary smiles. "Tomorrow is low tide."

---

Three people walk on the beach. Tide is out. A massive cliff looms—layers of rock and soil stacked like pages in a book.

Mr. Hartley kneels beside a pool. A crab moves across stone, claws extended.

"Look at the segmentation. The armored body." He glances at Mary. "You've found similar forms in the rock?"

"Similar. Older. Different species."

A gull lands near the pool. It approaches the crab and strikes down and cracks the shell with its beak. Then calls into the air.

Mr. Hartley watches. "We classify that gull by its beak structure, its feet, its smoothness against the wind. Observable traits. We don't need to hear its call or understand its thoughts."

Mary responds, "Everything needs to eat, shelter, procreate. We observe their features. What works tells us about the environment. Form reveals purpose."

Mr. Webb unwraps the ammonite fossil. Holds it up. "This shell—it controlled buoyancy. The opening suggests tentacles. The size suggests a predator."

"The form tells the story," Mary says. "Even when the creature is stone."

Mr. Hartley pauses. "Your work will make much noise at the Geological Society, Miss Anning."

"They don't accept the poor or women as members."

"I know. Regardless, your work will be known there."

Silence.

Mr. Webb looks at the cliff face, as they all start to climb it.

Mary says, "Careful, gentlemen. The ground looks settled. But it can change very quickly. And the precipice is closer than it looks."

Three people dig.

### Lessons

This Maryland summer is so hot. We open the windows. Fill the pitchers. Dust the carpets. 

The house becomes disorganized.

I will tidy up. I ask the nice boy to visit the store for me. We need potatoes, fruits, sugar, and other supplies. I write him a shopping list.

"I can't read, ma'am".

"Can't read?" I say back. Everyone should read. How can you work if you can't read? It feels wrong that you cannot. "I'll teach you. We'll do it together."

He sits beside me at the kitchen table. He is sounding out words like any child would. Eager. Bright. We stop our chores for a few hours and I teach. 

After weeks we have our habits. He goes to get our supplies. We tidy the home and finish our chores. Then we take lessons on reading. It feels good to help him learn. He takes to it well.

My husband Hugh comes home early one day. Watches us. His face darkens.

"Boy. Leave the room."

I look up in surprise as the lesson ends. "Why are you ending the lesson?" I ask.

"You can't teach him to read."

"But Hugh, he's doing so well—"

"That's exactly why you have to stop. We could get in trouble."

My husband sits down. Speaks low, but firm. I did something wrong.

"Learning will spoil him. Once a slave can read, he is no longer a slave. He has eyes outside the household. He sees what we have and don't share. He thinks. He calculates. He plans. You'll ruin him for us. And it's against the law."

I say, "But every kid learns to read."

"This isn't Ohio or Pennsylvania. Our property rights aren't cruel, Sophia. They're order. Without them, chaos. They do the work. We do the thinking."

He touches my shoulder. Gentle.

"I know you mean well. But knowledge unfits a child to be a slave. Never do that again. It's wrong. For everyone. Frederick is clever. Proud. He tricked you."

I had been tidying up while Hugh talked. Why was I cleaning?

I take a minute to realize it all.

I close the book. I'm so stupid. He's right. Hugh knows how the world works. I'm an outsider, naive. Frederick is from the South. He took advantage of me. My slave took advantage of me! 

I leave the room. I look at him standing there. He's looking at me. He was listening to us! He's so tricky. The boy looks at me still. He wants help. Never again!

I tear his lesson book up. Tear every page into pieces. Never again!

He does the fetching. He does the tidying. He does the cleaning. I look over to make sure the work gets done. That is the order of things.

Years pass. The kitchen is stocked. The house is tidy and clean. I give orders. I read and write and go to parties. This is good. Everything is working.

One day Frederick never comes back. I don't know how he managed to flee North. To read maps. To read schedules. He's tricky.

I hear Frederick gave speeches in the North. We'll send snatchers after him.

I see Frederick wrote: _Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave_. Three books in all. They are popular. They sell many prints. That is our money.

I hear Frederick is speaking in Britain! I've never been to Europe.

The North is invading. Frederick told the world lies about our society. We must defend our rights.

The war is on. We will win.

The North builds rifles, cannons, ironclads, railroads, telegraph lines. Their workers make the designs, run the machines, send the orders down the wire. Farm boys become soldiers and factory hands.

We can fight better. We have justice on our side. We have cotton, metals, wood, powder. We have the brains. We have the hands.

We print the manuals. Create loading drills. Make signal codes and telegraph keys. We hand them to our fighters and our workers.

"To arms, boys".

"Form up, men".

"For hearth and home".

"I can't read, ma'am".

"I can't read, ma'am".

"I can't read, ma'am".

### Hands On Learning

I walk through the hospital one more time. It's true. The evidence is here walking, or dying, inside these walls.

Doctors' ward: one in ten new mothers die of childbed fever. Midwives' ward: one in twenty-five.

I watch the other doctors come from the autopsy room. Hands still stinking of death. They examine the mothers. Insert their fingers to check dilation. Some mothers die three days later. Fever. Agony.

I tell my fellow doctors: wash your hands with chlorinated lime solution. 

"Quiet, Dr. Semmelweis, you Hungarian fool!"

I show them the numbers. "We are carrying death from the corpses to the mothers."

They stare at me with rage.

"You're saying we kill our patients? We heal them. We open the windows to bring in fresh air."

"I'm saying wash. Just wash."

Everyone responds, "How can putting lime on our hands possibly help patients, especially the ones with open wounds who need examination most. How? We are men of science! Please tell us!"

"I can't explain it. But aren't more living and fewer dead reason enough?"

I'm removed from the hospital. I write letters. I beg them. The numbers are clear. 

No one listens. No one washes.

They commit me to an asylum. Guards beat me yesterday. My hand is bleeding. The wound is green, oozing. I feel weak. Something is wrong.

Is it me? Aren't the numbers clear?

I am a medical doctor in Scotland, Dr. Lister.

I read Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation. He's in France. Yet I read his achievements within months of their publication. How wonderful. Decay of organic matter is not caused by spontaneous generation. Rather, germs in the air are the cause.

Germs. Tiny little lifeforms. I guess we can't see the details of comets, planets, and moons until we zoom in. I guess we can't see germs either. But both are real nonetheless.

If germs cause fermentation, they can cause infection.

I think of carbolic acid. It's used to treat wood from rot. I wash the wound of a patient in carbolic acid. I wrap it with plaster. No help.

But I will keep going.

I experiment with other forms of carbolic acid, in lotion, lint, oil. I apply carbolic acid to wounds before surgery. Spray it in the air. Soak the instruments.

Some wounds don't fester. More patients survive.

I publish the results. Some colleagues adopt it. Others resist.

"Your techniques are too much trouble," they say. "Unproven, Dr. Lister."

Several saved. The evidence talks.

It works. I've seen it work. I talk to the people who are alive because of it. Germs are small. Tiny. Carbolic acid kills these germs. Infections do not generate spontaneously. Clear air does not heal patients sickness. The evidence is mounting. Hospitals in Germany, in France—they're adopting antisepsis. Death rates are falling. Mothers are living.

Dozens saved. Scientific papers are written. The evidence talks.

Hand washing spreads.

President Garfield is shot.

I Dr. Bliss am the attending physician. I take charge. The bullet is lodged somewhere in his abdomen. I need to find it. I've been a surgeon for thirty years. I know wounds. I will save the president. He is a Union war hero. A good man.

### USA - More Chances

Paul Baran
Engineer, Drexel Institute of Technology

1955-. The Soviets detonate a truly massive hydrogen bomb. Much of my family is still in Eastern Europe. I'm in the United States. Nuclear war must be prevented.

Paper, mail, and phone calls are slow.

Computers are new, powerful, and advancing quickly. I should know, I worked at Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation on UNIVAC models. I'm getting my masters in engineering from UCLA. I have so many resources. I have so much responsibility. I can help prevent nuclear war.

Must get started.

1959-. I'm a researcher at RAND Corporation in Santa Monica. The Air Force sends us a list of problems. I scan it. Pick one: "communication survival in nuclear attacks." Most American military communications uses high-frequency connections. Those are vulnerable to the radiation of nuclear attacks. Communication blackouts mean itchy atomic trigger fingers. Resilient communication networks are faster, more reliable and therefore prevent nuclear war.

Must keep going.

1960-. Fact! A human brain can recover lost functions by bypassing a dysfunctional area.

Idea! A completely distributed communication network. With no centralization information can always find a way through. Like a fishnet with every node connected to multiple others. Run simulations with my team. Discovery! Just three or four connections per node are nearly as robust as the theoretical limit. Redundancy is possible. Redundancy is key.

Problem! Our phone system has physical connections. Phone connections therefore fail and drop calls rather easily. We need to make communications digital and redundant. Let's break every message into very small blocks of data. Each finds its own route through the net. Everything reassembles at the destination. Like sending a book in postcards.

If a message is lost, we can send just the page needed, not the entire book. Block messages vastly increase redundancy.

Must keep going.

1962-. I'm an engineer. I have a team. We draw diagrams. Write papers. Detailed. Very detailed. RAND presents to the Air Force. Approved!

Let's keep going.

The Defense Department wants this network for all military branches. On command, I bring it to the Defense Communication Agency. Bureaucratic mess. Delays. Jurisdictional concerns. Inertia.

Must keep going.

I try AT&T. They are a monopoly. They control the entire phone network. Just needs some modifications. I present the system. Switching, routing, block messages, redundancy protocols, error correction.

Eyes roll. At me. At the distributed network diagrams. Analog engineer approaches me, takes chalk from my hand, and says "Son, this is how a telephone works." Then ninety-four separate speakers describe the AT&T phone system. An executive says "Now do you see why block messages won't work?"

I look at him.

"No."

Must keep going.

1964-. RAND lets me publish eleven volumes of engineering specifications. Complete system architecture. Anyone can read them. US government's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) distributes the volumes at conferences. A Brit, Donald Davies, has a similar idea. He called message blocks "packets". It's a new name for a new idea. I like it.

1967-. Larry Roberts reads our work. He works at ARPA. He approves funding. Vint Cerf, Robert Kahn, other grad students are building the hardware and software to send digital packets. They are doing great work. The distributed communication network is called ARPAnet.

1969-. Four locations are online. UCLA to Stanford Research Institute. UC Santa Barbara to University of Utah.

1971-. Eighteen nodes are connected. Ray Tomlinson invents email. People love it.

1973-. Forty nodes. International connections start up.

The fishnet keeps growing.

No center to destroy. No single point of failure. No control possible.

We are going.

Newspapers. Textbooks. Whitepapers. Emails. People are writing about the network. People are using the network everyday from government, military, schools, now even some regular homes are online.

Three more nodes connect.

### Story—Not unique

**DING...DING...DING...Thank you for calling claims, how can I help you?**

Most claims had one or more other drivers involved. So we talked to other adjusters constantly.

An easy way to relate to these colleagues was to ask, "What's your pending?"

Translation: how many claims do you have open? The higher the pending, the higher the daily grind of the adjuster.

Mine was usually 50–80. Some other insurance companies had 200 and a hold time of an hour. You'd hear it in their voice.

I'd talk to these adjusters and realize my department was actually one of the better ones out there. We mostly got along. We went on trips, did bar nights, celebrated each other's achievements, and respected most of our managers.

But they, and everyone, were simply up against a wall.

We had no control over the stream of problems we encountered, but since "insurance" is associated with making problems go away, a person running a red light, or getting their car stolen, became our problem to help solve.

One of my claims happened in another state. I couldn't find that state's regulations anywhere. I searched, I called around, and only days later did I finally get an answer.

In that state, a not-at-fault driver could sue the at-fault driver for a maximum of one thousand dollars. Anything above that, you cover yourself if you have collision coverage. My insured didn't. So his car was wrecked by someone else's mistake, and he was paying for almost all of it.

I had to explain that over a long phone call.

But whose fault was it? He hadn't bought collision coverage. But nobody had told him what that could mean in another state. I'd helped process thousands of claims and I didn't know the situation was even possible.

The point is, after asking "what's your pending?" to hundreds of adjusters around the country, these stories were common. With so many different regulations, laws, policies, vehicles, companies, etc. Complications and surprises are inevitable.

Here's another example to illuminate the point. 

For decades, every American car shared the same handful of standardized headlight designs—universal, cheap, and always in stock. But the sealed-beam technology was getting old. When deregulation came in 1983, innovation arrived with it—halogen, then LED—but then ran away entirely.

Basically, we didn't upgrade standard parts and make new kinds of products to fill different niches. Instead, we used better robotics and design software to diversify parts to recreate the same end-products. 

Today there are thousands of unique headlight assemblies, each specific to one model and trim level, and priced accordingly:

In 1982 a sealed beam cost about fifty dollars in today's money. You bought it at any parts store, it fit almost anything on the road, and you swapped it yourself in ten minutes.

In 2026 the cost is four hundred to four thousand or more on a premium car. Installation likely means pulling the bumper, which adds several hundred more in labor costs. And the worst part, since most cars have their own version, there are many different headlights. Instead of easily stocked shelves there's often weeks on back-order.

And this problem is just for headlights. The same complexity exists throughout automobile supply chains, regulations, contracts, and so on. The increasing complication of our economy has strong ripple effects. Who pays for the increased costs? Whoever does means higher prices for cars, repairs, replacement parts, and insurance premiums. Arguing over who pays is now routine and definitely raises insureds' emotions and adjusters' pendings.

Modern LED headlights are better. But there's no reason they can't be more standardized and easier to install. 

Just food for thought. These insurance stories are not about my job. The bell ringing at the claims center isn't only a warning for adjusters to work faster—it's ringing for you too, a signal about the state of your economy, heard directly from the frontline.

### Story—Cured

You might remember: one of my first claims involved tracking down a Buick Allure for my insured. I did it, but it took hours that I didn't have to spend. Here's the thing, she never actually asked. She'd simply said, very nicely mind you: "I only drive Buick Allures." I assumed it was my job, so I found one.

Years later, I had a similar conversation.

She said, "I only drive Land Rovers."

Her tone wasn't nice. I kept typing out her repair and rental requisition orders.

"Hello?"

"Hi." I responded.

"I only drive Land Rovers...Did you hear me?" 

That's the trick of bullies: they never ask. They state. Asking implies the other person's agency—it allows compromise or disappointment. I learned silence was the best response. After all, she wasn't actually speaking to me.

"I said I only drive Land Rovers."

I knew what she wanted: for me to accept this as fact. But I'm not a valet, nor a rental company. Besides, rental coverage is about 30 dollars a year. The idea that we'd have an $80,000 SUV at the ready for her is ridiculous.

"You can ask the rental company about that."

"Then why do I buy insurance?"

Every adjuster's favorite question. I'd learned the best response by then.

"I don't know. Why did you buy this rental insurance?"

I said it gently. I was having a conversation; she was being the bully.

"I can't believe this. This isn't my problem." The bell was probably going DING DING DING by the way.

"With respect, if you want to drive a Land Rover as a rental I don't know of any company that stocks those. They usually opt for reliable cars with good resale values."

"Can I speak with your manager?"

"I can ask him." 

"Ask him!"

"I can't make him call you." I continued, "Insurance deals with contracts and policies. He'd probably ask where it says you can only rent Land Rovers. Every policy is contract and they don't specify brands. I can send your authorization for a rental in that class, depending on availability. Or I can send you a cheque and you arrange your own rental, uber, or public transportation."

She took the pre-authorized rental. I never heard from her again.

### Discovery

On August 24th, year 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted. The volcano pushed ash clouds miles into the sky. Soon scorching debris was falling for miles around like snow during a fierce Siberian blizzard. Thereafter, Italian towns Pompeii and Herculaneum were frozen in a single, catastrophic moment. A snapshot of Roman life was preserved under endless ash—everything from bread in ovens to human expressions of terror, from artistic frescoes to fragile scrolls.

Yet this moment of destruction was just another period of profound loss.

Even with all the many ruins we can visit today, most of everything from the ancient world is gone. For example, historians estimate that over 99% of ancient literature was lost to time—burned, destroyed, disintegrated, or simply forgotten. Entire libraries, generations of human knowledge, vanished like the smoke of Mount Vesuvius. The Library of Alexandria, with its hundreds of thousands of scrolls, would become the most famous casualty of this sad intellectual loss.

But some fragments survived, often in garbage dumps, old churches, or disaster sites like Pompeii and Herculaneum.

Covered by ash, the scrolls from Pompeii and Herculaneum were far too fragile to ever be opened. So we did not open them. They were collected and stored. It was left to our imaginations to dream of the knowledge that was locked inside.

Until, like the microscope let us see unknown tiny worlds, modern CT scanners could finally peer inside the ash scrolls, and machine learning algorithms could decode the information the scans uncovered. Now, after nearly 2000 years, we could finally read this time capsule of information.

Even so, Dr. Elena Stavros hadn't expected much when she ran the first fragment through the advanced CT scan. After endless hours in her lab she kept thinking "Another piece of ancient debris; another dead end". But something in one of the algorithmic reconstructions dazzled her eye.

Lines of text emerged. The machine learning model peeled back layers of carbon and time, revealing Greek characters that seemed to shimmer like fairy dust on her computer monitor.

"It's not just text," she whispered to her research team. "It's a new dialogue!"

The manuscript was from Magos of Samos—a name barely mentioned in historical records. We only know he, or she, was a student of Aristotle whose work had been lost to time. But here, preserved in the volcanic tomb of Pompeii was a text fragment.

A text fragment written not just to the future, but about the future.

The AI had done more than decode text. It had resurrected a voice that had been waiting nearly two millennia to be heard. Magos' words, preserved by volcanic fire, decoded by machine intelligence, were a lost story of Socrates learning a vital lesson that was remarkably prescient today.

Only fragments have been uncovered thus far, so the author filled in some gaps. It proved to be the best foreword the author and reader could ask for.

He tells himself someone had to be first to hear it, why not a soldier who is leaving the city for months if not years. Zographos then finds the courage to look at this man, Socrates.

Who looks back with the thrill of approaching understanding. "Go on. Changed how?"

Zographos sets down his wax tablet. Would this Socrates be his first student?

What nerves this thought has.

"You ask the right question, my friend!" Zographos says, embracing this chance. His words tumbling out with the enthusiasm of a man who has been alone with revolutionary thoughts for too long. "Watch." Zographos lifted a cage with a small finch. "This bird came from a land with only hard seeds. Now look—" He lifts another finch. "but this one lived where only soft insects were available. Its beak became slender and precise."

"You're saying the islands..."

"Each island shaped them differently," Zographos continues, now just speaking his thoughts, his shoulders drop and tension falls, this feels liberating. "The same ancestor, but each population adapted! That's the book! The dry islands demanded one solution, the wet islands another."

With poised vigour he continues "And the Gods selected the ones who were to breed on." 

Socrates stares at the birds, his mind racing. "And you believe this principle applies to island creatures?"

Zographos' eyes gleam. "My friend, I believe this principle explains all creatures."

Socrates strokes his weather leather straps, and grips his spear, then nods to encourage Zographos to continue.

"You care for me to continue. You are interested?"

"It's nice to meet someone who has been away for many years. I think it is fair to say I have a reputation for curiosity, as I am sure you will find out. I perhaps have enough to make a difference to our fair city's future."

Zographos looks directly at Socrates, his voice dropping to an almost reverent whisper. "The whole process—the inheritance, the variation, the selection, the branching into new forms to cast away the unneeded old—I call it evolution."

Socrates feels something shift deep within his mind, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known existed. The questions that had plagued him about justice, virtue, and human nature suddenly reorganize themselves around this single, powerful idea. If humans, like these creatures, were shaped by the challenges they faced...

The horn blows thrice.

Socrates holds his spear, and old armour, and leans in and says with a smile, "Tell me more about evolution, I think we shall put it to use before long".

Zographos hesitated, knowing the complexity of life that he had witnessed. "It will take generations," he said, "to truly understand what these islands have revealed."

Socrates smiled, holding his weathered spear. "When I return we will get started," he responded, "and set the foundations for our ancestors to complete the job when their time comes."

### Explainer

Sadly, there was no Magos of Samos. There are plenty of scrolls found at Pompeii and Herculaneum. Scientists are just now starting to use CT scanners and machine learning to unlock their contents. So far, no lost ancient works have been found.

Alas, the scene you just read is a fictional thought experiment—a glimpse into an alternate history where two of humanity's most transformative intellectual revolutions occurred simultaneously.

I used it to teach a vital lesson.

First however, we must realize why Socrates (470–399 BC) is so important. He was, as the Roman philosopher Cicero observed, the first to "call philosophy down from heaven, place it in cities and homes, and compel it to inquire about life and morals as well as good things and bad." Before him, the common practice was to let professional debaters, known as Sophists, battle over a question. Everyone assumed justice had no universal definition and was merely a matter of law, custom, or power. Sophists used rhetoric to convince people that their side served the ruler's interests and was therefore just.

Socrates challenged this mindset. Instead of accepting that justice was relative to human laws or customs, he asked things like “What is justice?” or "What is ethics".

Such deceptively simple questions were in line with ancient Greek natural philosophers such as Thales, Anaximander, and Pythagoras. These thinkers discovered natural laws of geometry, mathematics, and observation that helped craftsmen and engineers to calculate volume, weight, and angles more precisely, thereby empowering Greek civilization to build increasingly sophisticated sewers, ships, buildings, and countless other wonders.

Socrates asked why wouldn't human society also be subject to universal laws if water, ships, and engineering are?

Socrates, and his best student Plato (427-347 BC), thus turned philosophy towards the human condition with practical questions about how we should live, what constitutes justice, and how societies should be organized. Socrates taught his students to ask these questions, in what we now call the Socratic method, in order to use philosophy to build a better person and society the same way engineers use mathematicians to build better buildings.

Plato wrote these lessons down, adding his own thoughts along the way, in books called Socratic dialogues. By forever studying these works, every subsequent development in Western philosophy, politics, and ethics is rooted in this Socratic revolution.

Charles Darwin (Zographos), over two millennia later, revealed the mechanism by which all life adapts and evolves. Although many scientists laid the groundwork for Darwin, their contribution was often removing the buildup of erroneous human narratives, such as all animals being created as they exist today and the Earth only being a few thousand years old.

While it was never going to be easy, Darwin's insights into natural selection, common descent, and evolutionary adaptation were always there to be discovered. After all, farmers long knew much about inheritance among plants and animals because they bred them so often. Even so, it took a very fortunate moment, like visiting the isolated Galapagos Islands for Darwin, or the Canary Islands for Zographos, to display natural selection and adaptation simply enough to seed evolution in someone's mind.

When life's evolution was realized, it quickly transformed our understanding of the origin of species, including ourselves, in the natural world.

So, what if these two intellectual revolutions had happened together? What if Darwin's insight on evolution had been made, accepted, and popularized along with Ancient Greek contributions like Thales' geometric principles, Euclid's mathematical foundations, and Archimedes' engineering innovations.

This thought experiment says if Socrates, or one of his followers like the hypothetical Magos, had known about evolution he would have made an important connection—mankind's evolution now runs through tools as well as biology. Thus, the mathematical and engineering foundations Socrates and his students loved weren't only inspirational but intertwined with human affairs because the technical and human domains co-evolve.

It's entirely possible Socrates or his students would have made the connection that because evolution was a natural law for biology, then it must be for humans and our technology as well.

Surrounded by far more wilderness, ancient Greeks cherished their language, plays, ships, trade, and civilization. Wilderness was seen as a dangerous realm that required a civilized hero to subdue. Moreover Greeks were extremely adventurous, competitive, warlike, and commercial. Many people experienced tragedies such as shipwrecks, plagues, or sieges that made technology's value as the differentiator between survival and flourishing readily apparent.

Thus, Greeks might have naturally accepted technological evolution as the guiding principle of their fate. They already had tool-using heroes such as Achilles and Odysseus but explained their success because of mythical abilities instead of their willingness to drop old tools and master new ones as needed; famously seen when Odysseus built the Trojan horse to finally conquer Troy after failing for a decade to take the walled city by traditional siege warfare.

What the Greeks lacked was an understanding of evolution as a universal law.

This led them, and thus us, down an inaccurate trail, the same way not knowing universal gravitation led Plato's student Aristotle (384–322 BC) to create an Earth-centered universe that guided humanity down the wrong path for centuries.

Interestingly, Aristarchus of Samos (310-230 BC) advocated for a Sun-centered universe. He even calculated the relative sizes of the Sun, Moon, and Earth. Still, Aristotle's ideas won out and became orthodoxy. After rediscovering an old scroll of Aristarchus, Nicholas Copernicus (1473-1543) would later re-establish the Sun-centered model of astronomy in the early 1500s. With this in mind, it's not impossible that an ancient Darwin could have existed among the 99% of ancient knowledge that was lost.

If early thinkers like Socrates, Plato, Aristotle had grasped the principle that humans and their tools co-evolve in response to environmental challenges—how might they have approached questions of ethics, politics, and justice?

Imagine the changes to our history, filled with empires that rise and fall, racism, sexism, slavery, recessions, economic and population booms and collapses, revolutions, civil wars, and so much more we hate but keep coming back?

Perhaps these recurring patterns aren't moral failures or inevitable human flaws, but symptoms of civilizations struggling to adapt to technological changes we don't yet understand. Instead of asking "How do we eliminate war?" or "How do we achieve perfect justice?" an evolutionary Socrates would ask: "What technological pressures create these conflicts?", "How do societies smoothly adapt to new tools and capabilities?", and perhaps most importantly, "How do we teach our kids born with biological instincts to best live in a technological world?"

This reframing doesn't excuse human suffering or injustice, but it offers a different path forward. Rather than assuming our future depends on "bringing down from heaven" superior laws, policies, or moral education, we might focus on understanding how technological ecosystems can evolve toward greater human flourishing—just as Darwin's finches evolved better beaks for their particular island environment rich in either insects, nuts, or seeds.

The interaction between Socrates and our fictional Darwin/Zographos represents the birth of what we might call "evolutionary philosophy", "biology for humans", or "techvolution"—a framework for understanding human societies as technological ecosystems that adapt, evolve, and thrive through the same principles that govern all life.

Yet, this story did not happen.

Socrates and Darwin never met, the same way Aristotle and Newton never did. But this tale serves as food for thought because you know Socrates and Darwin, so despite their distance from each other, they are meeting in you. And that is the vital lesson this short story was meant to teach. The stream of events we call history is not pre-organized for us. In order to advance our story we must be the ones to the connect dots that should be connected. Thus Magos, the thinker who connected Socrates and Darwin, can be real in you.

Why not make it so.

The connection waits for whoever picks it up. 

Despite the lofty goals the Techvolution series is setting out to achieve, we are only adding some of our scientific knowledge to the foundational questions Socrates first posed about human civilization.

That way, humans will correct the path we've been living by applying evolutionary thinking to understand how civilizations adapt via technological change. This will help us consciously guide our civilization's evolution toward a future where people will no longer have to ask "what is justice" because they'll instead be intentionally building it.
