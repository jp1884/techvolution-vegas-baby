
# Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species

## Book Series—Prologue

## Opening

"Technology is anything that was not around when you were born."

—Alan Kay

## Author's Note

This book does not tell you politics and economics are getting really bad, and to fix it, here's what _someone else_ needs to change.

This book explains why politics and economics are getting both good and bad—and how you, the reader, can adapt to use the good to fix the bad.

In other words, this is a book for people in the middle. The people who want things to be more rational, level-headed, future-focused, and are willing to work for it. I hope you like it.

## Giving You a Map and Compass

It is 2011, and a woman named Maria has built a good life. Californian, Hispanic, college graduate, progressive, movie lover, book nerd, regional manager—she carries all the labels we commonly use to describe a person. But none of them captures the fact that matters most to her future: high-speed internet has finally become widespread, and Maria manages distribution for a company that rents and sells Digital Video Discs (DVDs).

Physical media has a powerful challenger. Maria's professional networks, her hard-won expertise, her entire livelihood—all of it could lose its value. But DVD players sit in nearly every TV stand in America. Families have spent years and real money filling their home theaters with media collections. Are they really going to "stream" their beloved shows and movies from far away data-centers instead?

Yes.

Where did Maria's pain come from? She made regional manager by pushing her company from VHS to DVDs, and customer traffic boomed. Why is this new technology causing her grief? 

The compact disc was born in the 1980s from a partnership between Dutch Philips and Japanese Sony. It stored a music album of data. The DVD followed a decade later holding an entire movie. The DVD spread to countless stores and homes—in capitalist countries and socialist ones, secular and religious neighborhoods, red and blue legislatures. Soon, discs with far better video and audio quality—HD DVD and Blu-ray—were brought to market.

People ignored them. Within a few years physical media sales fell off a cliff everywhere at once, regardless of nation, ideology, or economic system. Reflect on this situation: when you stopped buying DVDs, did you consult your identity or belief system first?

If you don't remember DVDs, ask your parents. In Milan or Milwaukee, Seoul or São Paulo, they'll tell you the same story: the Friday night trip to the video rental store, the delight of walking the aisles of selection, the shelf of cases by the TV. A shared memory across every border and belief—and a shared extinction as nearly everyone learned how to stream movies within a few years.

What could synchronize the lives of billions of strangers to totally upend Maria's future?

Notice what every standard explanation gets wrong. Blaming rental giants like Blockbuster for missing the boat doesn't explain why every other company missed it too. Blame government policy, and you can't explain why the same collapse happened in every country at once. Blame Maria, and you can't explain the thousands of other industry insiders who thought DVDs would remain king.

Yet, for Maria—and everyone else today—these are the identities and levers we use to explain the state of our finances, job prospects, and futures. None offers a universal and reproducible explanation.

But, one lens explains all three at once: a new technology found users who wanted what it offered. No villain decided Maria's fate. Technology and its users evolved. Maria was connected to an adaptation that was essentially killed off. Maria's livelihood and identity were standing in the path of a natural process that affects all lifeforms on Earth. Once we realize this is true in technology like in biology we see Maria was unprepared because no one had taught her to watch these forces, perhaps because everyone was too busy following them.

Maria is just one chapter in history—and the next chapter asks about you, today. 

Will AI take your job?

More importantly, *should* it? After all, you very likely switched from movie theaters, to DVDs, and then to streaming, drastically changing the fates of countless people.

So will others do the same and make you today's Maria?

You don't know. Like streaming before it, AI is an amazing new technology, and we don't yet know what changes it will bring despite the CD, DVD, and endless other examples of new technology transforming our fate.

That ignorance should strike you as strange. In physics, we can peer billions of years into the past and take pictures of individual atoms. In medicine, we can cure terrible diseases and watch cells divide in real time. We can do so much—so how is it that we can see so little of our own futures?

We don't even have a name for Maria's future, or yours, no label that might have told her what to expect and how to prepare.

And it's bigger than any one person, job, or company. Housing, healthcare, education, and childcare have consumed a growing share of the median paycheck for forty years, through boom and bust, whichever party held power. Meanwhile high-speed internet, automation, and AI keep getting better. How can people be worse off while using better technology? That is Maria's problem, scaled up to all of society.

We've been drowning in proposed answers, every day another ocean of speeches, articles, and posts promising the fix. We've listened, and our frustration has curdled into a growing hatred of our own countrymen. Decades of voting in new leaders and switching parties have added up to failure, not because we tried too little, but because we were aiming at the wrong target.

When a biologist sees an ecosystem failing, she doesn't get angry. She looks for the imbalance, too much of one creature, too little of another, and works to restore it. Our society has the same kinds of problems, but we still read politics and economics through human narratives. To see what would have warned Maria, and us, we first have to see technology for what it is: an extension of life's natural evolution. Thereafter, we can embrace life's game plan for prospering in an ever-changing world.

Evolve new species, better suited to the new environment.

This book won't hand you simple answers; I don't know your specific situation. It's written for the person who wants a decent paycheck for an honest day's work, and who'd happily learn a new skill to get it. Someone who'd compromise if it fixed anything, drop an old position if a better one came along, and doesn't want a handout or a bailout. Someone who'd rather get to work, go home, and enjoy their family, but keeps getting told to pick a side, when neither side is talking about the thing actually squeezing their paychecks, their time, and their kids' futures.

If that sounds like you, you're not confused, and you're not alone. You're just missing a name for what's happening behind the political bickering and the economic statistics.

Your paycheck is a species, subject to all the laws of nature. Some are thriving, some are going extinct, and nobody has told you which one yours is.

## Book Series

_Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species_ presents a different way of understanding human civilization: our fate is driven not by our beliefs or ideologies, but by the technologies we create and use. These ideas aren't meant to stay on the blackboard.

They're meant to build a working day where your tools are easier to learn, where changing jobs doesn't mean starting over, and where you match each problem with the best tool regardless of which ideological tribe it belongs to. Getting there takes three steps.

**Book I—A New Identity:** It starts with how we see ourselves. We have names for our nationality, our ethnicity, our job, our politics, but no name for the identity that quietly runs our lives: the tools we use to house ourselves, get around, and earn a living. Because we miss it, we blame ourselves, "the other" political party, or bad luck when the ecosystem shifts under our feet.

Evolution never saw Maria as a regional manager or a college graduate. It saw someone selling physical-media stories in the Internet Age. And, regardless of the stories we tell, slow adaptations get replaced in nature _and_ civilization. Someone who knows this can read the wind instead of cursing it, shed the aging tool, and transfer the hard-won knowledge to the next adaptation. After all, Maria's eye for a good story and her grip on logistics are needed in whatever comes next. As is the knowledge and experience you have today as AI challenges our place in the world.

**Book II—Mixed Ideological Politics:** Today our politics is a fight between stories, on the news and in podcasts. But underneath, most of us are defending the tools that pay our bills. The oil-field worker and the solar installer argue about the climate. The trucker and the transit planner argue about cities. Each is talking about values, and each is also protecting a livelihood. That's not hypocrisy; it's what every creature does. Once we've named the tools we depend on, we can argue about them honestly, instead of hiding them behind speeches and echo chambers.

We see a heat pump and think it's "green," or a diesel truck and think it's "red-blooded American." Once a tool wears a label, half of us won't touch it. Nobody gets the whole toolbox, and the whole ecosystem grows slower, if at all.

Nature doesn't function that way. Forests grow where the sun shines and the water runs. Book II teaches us how to strip story-based identities from our tools, so solar panels and diesel generators can go wherever they contribute, without anyone feeling they've betrayed a tribe. That freedom is the political moderation we need, reimagined for the Internet Age.

**Book III—Bumblebee Economics:** Free to choose our tools, we can finally build a better working day. Everyone needs food, water, and shelter, and to get them we create power, transfer its energy to make products and distribute them. Nature already knows how to make that energy flow work reliably. Every plant can grow because every plant uses the same sunlight. Predators eat most meats. Decomposers break down any lifeform. A bee works any flower it finds. When the inputs are universal, everyone gets to play, and nature finds the best fit for opportunity and lifeform.

This means we need far more standards. We're good at making standards like USB, shipping containers, and VIN numbers. Less good at settling on them: the DVD's successor foolishly split into two rival and more expensive discs. And we have few standards at all for selling, staffing, or moving goods and people. Just think of the pains of resume writing, interviews, onboarding, and learning the ropes of a new job, never mind a new industry.

With coherent standards, Maria's next niche is easier to find and embrace. She can use her knowledge and skill sets to cross-pollinate a new part of the ecosystem. Book III details the adaptations that enforce widespread standards and interoperability in nature so we can use them in our economy. When every organism is put to constant use, nothing is wasted. Bounty is the result in nature, and in civilization too.

`Understand yourself → Learn to select better tools → Build bounty with others`
