
# Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species

## Author's Note

This book series does not add to the culture wars by saying healthcare, housing costs, or anything else is getting terrible, and to fix it, here's what _someone else_ needs to change.

Our ideologies are made for an earlier time: some brilliant, all imperfect. So, these books are for the people who want to use them as guides to be more rational, level-headed, and future-focused, and who are willing to adapt to improve our imperfect world.

## Giving You a Map and Compass

It is 2008, and a woman named Maria has built a good life. Californian, Hispanic, college graduate, progressive, movie lover, book nerd, distribution manager—she carries all the labels we commonly use to describe a person. But none of them captures the fact that matters most to her future: high-speed internet has finally become widespread, and Maria handles the distribution for a DVD rental company.

Physical media has a challenger. Maria's professional networks, her hard-won expertise, her entire livelihood—all of it could lose its value. But DVD players sit in nearly every TV stand in America. Families have spent years and real money filling their home theaters with media collections. Are they really going to "stream" their beloved shows and movies from far away data centers instead?

Yes.

Increasingly as families gather on friday nights, Maria's shelves are half full. Her favorite customers don't come to talk film history. And worst of all, she must look into the yes of her workers after handing out less hours each week. Where did all this pain come from?

Maria started by running one store in a twenty-store VHS video rental chain. A film geek, she convinced head office in the mid-90s that despite DVD players high-cost, their better quality, clarity, and compact size, were the next big thing for their customers. Her passion won her control of the company's distribution warehouse, where her eye for a good story told her what movies should be stocked in what neighborhoods. Foot traffic surged and her company grew to thirty stores. Maria had bet on a new technology and won.

So why is new technology causing her grief?

Because DVDs and streaming are from different branches on the technolgy tree, and they are fighting for the same niche. 

DVDs come from plastic storage discs were possible because of material science and laser discoveries in the 1950s and 60s. Together they wrote and read pits on plastic like letters in a book. The consumer technology was born in the 1980s when the CD stored a music album of data. The DVD followed a decade later holding an entire movie, and soon competed against the widely the popular VHS video tape.

Within a few years the plastic discs were in countless stores and homes—in capitalist countries and socialist ones, secular and religious neighborhoods, left or right-leaning cities and towns. Soon, discs with far better video and audio quality—HD DVD and Blu-ray—were brought to market as the next big thing. Each was displayed next to the bigger and better TVs people kept buying.

Some people the better disc. Not many and soon enough physical media sales soon fell off a cliff in country after country, regardless of ideology or economic system. Ask yourself, when you stopped using DVDs, did you consult your identity or belief system first?

If you don't remember DVDs, ask your parents. In Milan or Milwaukee, Seoul or São Paulo, they'll tell you the same story: the Friday night trip to the video rental store, the delight of walking the aisles of selection, the shelf of DVD cases by the TV. A shared memory across every border and belief—and a shared extinction as nearly everyone learned how to stream movies within a few years.

What could synchronize the lives of billions of strangers to totally upend Maria's future?

Notice what every standard explanation gets wrong. Praising Netflix doesn't mean you can repeat their success. Blaming rental giants like Blockbuster for missing the boat doesn’t explain why so many other companies missed it too. Blame government policy, and you can't explain why the same collapse happened in every country. Blame Maria, and you can't explain the thousands of other industry insiders who thought the staggered release schedule of movie theater, DVDs, cable television would keep the world fixated on Hollywood's content.

Yet, for Maria these are the explanations she had to make sense of her finances, job prospects, and future. Everyone today relies on similar outlooks none offers a universal and reproducible explanation.

But one fundamental explains it all. Like media discs, high-speed internet has it's own development history. When speeds finally got fast enough to fill a television screen on demand, people got what they wanted all along. No thoughts. No feelings. They made the switch by scrating an itch, and moved on with their lives.

No personal failure or ideological villain decided Maria's fate. Maria identity as distribution manager was a name-tag. In reality, she was someone selling physical media in the Internet Age. As, technology and its users naturally evolved, the DVD distribution manager was connected to an adaptation that was essentially killed off. 

And, regardless of the stories we tell ourselves, and the name-tags we write, losing adaptations naturally get replaced. This is true in technology just as it is in biology. Maria's livelihood and identity were standing in the path of a natural process that affects all lifeforms on Earth. No one had taught her to expect, watch, measure, and prepare for these impersonal forces. Her pain needn't look so obvious only in retrospect.

Maria is just one chapter in history—and the next chapter asks about you, today.

Will AI take your job?

More importantly, *should* it? After all, you very likely switched from movie theaters, to VHS, to DVDs, and then to streaming, drastically changing the fates of countless people.

So will others do the same and make you today's Maria?

You don't know. Like streaming before it, AI is new technology. But even after the CD, the DVD, digital cameras, air-conditioning, trains, and countless other technologies transformed our lives, we still can't see what change AI will bring.

That ignorance should strike you as strange. In physics, we can peer billions of years into the past and aim a rocket that lands rovers on Mars. In medicine, we can watch cells divide in real time and test if blood has enough iron to carry oxygen around a body. We can know so much in these fields—so how is it that we can see so little of our own political and economic futures that whether our job will exist next year is an open question?

We don't even have a name for Maria's fate, or yours. She was moving DVDs, but what society was her work building? What is your job building? What are we all building? We work most of our lives and still have little idea what to expect and how to prepare as it grows and changes under our feet.

And it's bigger than any one person, job, or company. Housing, healthcare, education, and childcare have consumed a growing share of the median paycheck for forty years, through boom and bust, whichever party held power. Young people have been priced out of homes, retirees worry about money, and workers from healthcare to construction are held back from their jobs by mountains of administrative paperwork.

Meanwhile high-speed internet, automation, and AI keep getting better. How can people be concerned for their future while using ever improving technology? Forest dwellers don't worry when there's more leaves to feed the ecosystem. That is Maria's uncertainty, scaled up to all of society.

We've been drowning in proposed answers, every day another ocean of speeches, articles, and posts promising the fix. We've listened, and our frustration has curdled into a growing hatred of our own countrymen. Decades of voting in new leaders and switching parties have not brought enough solutions, not because we tried too little, but because we were aiming at the wrong target.

When a biologist sees struggling lifeforms or an ecosystem failing, she doesn't get angry. She assesses the flow of energy, and looks for the imbalance—too much of one creature, too little of another, and then works to restore it. Our society has the same kinds of problems, but we still read our politics and economics through human narratives as if past ideas are all we need to solve the problem. Here’s what would have warned Maria, and us. The human story is primarily driven not by our beliefs or ideologies, but by the technologies we create and use. Our inventions take us along for a ride we don't quite appreciate yet. But we can, because technology is an extension of life’s natural evolution to harvest energy. 

We understand biology quite well. So we can understand how our paychecks are connected to a tool, which makes it a species subject to replacement like any other. For us, that means accepting our supply chain will evolve according to people's choices.

This book series won’t hand you simple answers; only you and people around you know your specific situation. Instead, you’ll get something Maria never had: a solid understanding of what’s happening to us and our environment in the young Internet Age, and the readiness to match your problems with tools that solve them.

Digital technology didn't just transform television watching. With every screen, monitor, and processor, our entire planet is changing very fast. We are like elephants at the start of an Ice Age, adapted for a certain climate, but now encountering a totally different one for the first time. The frustration and anger you feel, and see in others, aren't signs that the other side is broken. They're symptoms of a shared problem: we don't yet know how to read the new environment modern technology has built. But, our ancestors faced this many times, and their history guides us to what mindsets work and what don't. And happily, we can borrow from modern sciences like biology and ecology, to begin solving our problems before failure sets in.

Maria didn't need a prophecy. Her next niche is easier to find if she realized we are no longer using tools to harvest from wild nature, but rather building an entire technological ecosystem that sustains us. With this self-awarenmess Maria might be more scared to ask "what if AI takes no ones job?". After all, being part of a technologicaly stagnate society is hardly a recipe for security. The internet faced stiff resistance from phone companies for decades. It took decades for doctors to sterilize their surgical tools. What housing prices or insurance costs pains might AI heal if we know where to adopt it?

There's jobs in this future. After all new technology offers new niches. The faster telecommunications that killed the DVD also enabled online shoping, which required Maria's skills of distribution. Or, she could use her storytelling skills with the explosion of education, entertainment, and other streaming content on Youtube, Instagram and Netflix.

we know biological evolution doesn't stop. Hoping it technological evolution does isn't a plan. What Maria, and you, need is a map of the game all life is playing, and a compass to tell her which way the board was moving.

That's what this book's new perspective gives you. The map is the Game of Life: the rules every player has to learn to stay in the game. The compass is a new identity: knowing your role and place in the ecosystem, so you can feel the ground shift and know which way to step.

And the name for what happened to Maria, and what’s happening to all of us, is techvolution: our tools evolve, and they take us along for the ride.

Let's see how the game is played.

## Book Series

_Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species_ presents a different way of understanding human civilization: the human story is primarily driven not by our beliefs or ideologies, but by the technologies we create and use. Our inventions take us along for a ride we don't quite appreciate yet.

This is a new perspective meant to build a better world, one where your tools will be easier to learn, changing jobs won't mean starting over, and matching problems at work, home, or school with a better tool is far simpler. Reaching this future means starting an update of three connected realms: individual identity, political discourse, and economic systems.

**Book I—A New Identity:** We already have names for our nationality, ethnicity, job, and politics, but no name for the identity that quietly runs our lives: the tools we use to house ourselves, get around, and earn a living. Because we miss it, we blame ourselves, “the other” political party, or bad luck when the ecosystem shifts under our feet.

Book I is less about solutions than about clearing our view, wiping away the fog of old stories that blinds us to the political compromises and economic fixes we could be adopting en masse. This future awaits once we see that, in evolutionary terms, Maria was never a distribution manager. She was someone selling physical media in the Internet Age. When we identify by our toolset, we'll see that technological ecosystems have an energy flow like any other, find our roles within it, and know where our skills and money fit as AI, and whatever comes next, reshape our future.

**Book II—Mixed Ideological Politics:** A heat pump doesn't know it's “green.” A diesel truck doesn't know it's “red-blooded American.” We attach such labels with stories, then reject good tools for wearing the wrong one. With our view cleared in Book I, Book II kick-starts faster problem-solving by showing us how to strip story-based identities from any tool, so we can adopt what works without feeling we've insulted or betrayed any tribe.

In nature, forests grow where the sun shines and the water runs. Book II judges tools the same way: by how well they help a local ecosystem thrive, not by which playbook they belong to. This radical freedom to grow and shed tools as the environment demands is political moderation reimagined for the Internet Age.

**Book III—Bumblebee Economics:** With our view cleared in Book I and our choice of tools freed in Book II, Book III turns to the economy: how to make it more reliable and renewable, from the power we generate to the goods we make and move. Nature's supply chain is our model. All plants use the same sunlight. Predators can survive on almost any meat. A bee works whichever flower it finds. By using universal standards, everyone gets to compete, and nature finds the best lifeform to harvest any available energy.

This means we need far more standards in our economy. We're good at making standards like USB, shipping containers, and VINs. Less good at settling on them: the DVD's successor split into two rival discs. And we have few standards at all for selling, staffing, or moving goods and people. Just think of the pains of resume writing, interviews, and learning the ropes of a new job, never mind a new industry.

With coherent standards, Maria's next niche is easier to find and embrace. She can use her knowledge and skills to cross-pollinate a new part of the ecosystem. Book III shows how nature spreads and automatically enforces shared standards, so our economy can do the same. When every organism can easily work and contribute, little is wasted. The result is bounty in nature, and in civilization too.

`Understand yourself → Learn to select better tools → Build bounty with others`
