
# Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species

## Book Series—Prologue

## Opening

"Technology is anything that was not around when you were born."

—Alan Kay

## Author's Note

This book does not tell you politics and economics are getting really bad, and to fix it, here's what _someone else_ needs to change.

This book explains why politics and economics are getting both good and bad—and how you, the reader, can adapt to use the good to fix the bad.

In other words, this is a book for people in the middle. The people who want things to be more rational, level-headed, future-focused, and are willing to work for it. I hope you like it.

## Giving You a Map and Compass

It is 2011, and a woman named Maria has built a good life. Californian, Hispanic, college graduate, progressive, movie lover, book nerd, regional manager—she carries all the labels we commonly use to describe a person. But none of them captures the fact that matters most to her future: high-speed internet has finally become widespread, and Maria rents and sells Digital Video Discs (DVDs) for a living.

Physical media has a powerful challenger. Maria's professional networks, her hard-won expertise, her entire livelihood—all of it could lose its value. But DVD players sit in nearly every TV stand in America. Families have spent years and real money filling their home theaters with private home video collections. Are they really going to "stream" their beloved shows and movies from far away data-centers instead?

Yes.

Where did Maria's pain come from? She made her name driving store traffic by quickly moving from VHS tapes to DVDs. Why is this new technology causing her grief? 

The compact disc was born in the 1980s from a partnership between Dutch Philips and Japanese Sony. It stored a music album of data. The DVD followed a decade later holding an entire movie. The DVD spread to countless stores and homes—in capitalist countries and socialist ones, secular and religious, left and right. Soon, discs with far better video and audio quality—HD DVD and Blu-ray—were brought to market.

People ignored them. Within a few years physical media sales fell off a cliff everywhere at once, regardless of nation, ideology, or economic system. Reflect on this situation: when you stopped buying DVDs, did you consult your identity or belief system first?

If you don't remember DVDs, ask your parents. In Mumbai or Milwaukee, São Paulo or Seoul, they'll tell you the same story: the Friday night trip to the video rental store, the delight of walking the aisles of selection, the shelf of cases by the TV. A shared memory across every border and belief—and a shared extinction as nearly everyone learned how to stream movies within a few years.

What could synchronize the lives of billions of strangers to totally upend Maria's future?

Notice what every standard explanation gets wrong. Blaming rental giants like Blockbuster for missing the boat doesn't explain why every other company missed it too. Blame government policy, and you can't explain why the same collapse happened in every country at once. Blame Maria, and you can't explain the thousands of other industry insiders who thought DVDs would remain king.

Yet, for Maria—and everyone else today—these are the identities and levers we use to explain the state of our finances, job prospects, and futures. None offers a universal, agreed upon explanation.

But, one lens explains all three at once: a new technology found users who wanted what it offered. No villain decided Maria's fate. Technology and its users evolved. Maria was connected to an adaptation that was essentially killed off. Maria's livelihood and identity were standing in the path of a natural process. No one had taught her to watch for it, perhaps because everyone was too busy following it.

Maria is just one chapter in history—and the next chapter asks about you, today. 

Will AI take your job?

More importantly, *should* it? After all, you very likely switched from movie theaters, to DVDs, and then to streaming, drastically changing the fates of countless people.

So will others do the same and make you today's Maria?

You don't know. Like streaming before it, AI is an amazing new technology, and we don't yet know what changes it will bring despite the CD, DVD, and endless other examples available for study.

That ignorance should strike you as strange. In physics, we can peer billions of years into the past and take pictures of individual atoms. In medicine, we can watch cells divide in real time and cure terrible diseases. We can do so much—so how is it that we can see so little of our own futures?

We don't even have a name for Maria's future, or yours—no label that might have told her what to expect and how to prepare.

This is a common problem, and it is bigger than any one job. There are many economically uncertain and politically scared Maria's in today's society as housing, healthcare, education, and childcare have consumed a growing share of the median paycheck for forty years—through boom and bust, under every administration, whichever party held power. Young people can't afford homes. Retirees worry about money running out. Healthcare workers spend more time on paperwork than on patients. Small businesses watch giant corporations grow bigger still.

In this environment, we don't know if AI will take our jobs, we don't even know what to call our uncertainty, and yet technology like high-speed internet, robotic automation, and AI keeps getting so much better. How can people be worse off while using better technology?

That is Maria's problem, scaled up to all of society. 

We have been drowning in proposed answers—every day another ocean of speeches, articles, and posts promising the fix. We've listened, and our frustration has curdled into a growing hatred of our own countrymen. Decades of voting in new leaders, switching parties, and wishing for more bipartisanship have added up to failure—not because we tried too little, but because we were aiming at the wrong target.

This book won't tell you how to build a better world on Monday morning. Many business, marketing, and maker books already do that better. This book aims at something underneath: a way of seeing that makes the fights over identity, politics, and economics finally start to lose their grip on you.

Because when a biologist sees a faster horse going hungry, or a warmer bird getting cold, they don't get angry. They research and often find answers. For example, when an ecosystem stops working biologists know there is too much of one creature, too little of another. They find the imbalance and work to restore it. Our society has the same kinds of problems but our understanding of politics and economics is still based in human narratives.

To gain the kind of understanding that would have warned Maria, and us, of what was coming, we need to first see the technology changing our lives for what it is: an extension of life's natural evolution. With this clarity in mind, we can start repairing and building a healthier civilization again.

Without a new mindset we'll keep clinging to the aging lifestyles and toolsets we know: cars, textbooks, office buildings, strip malls, movie franchises. And we'll keep fighting over who has to pay for that aging. The good news is that life has faced this struggle many times before, and the game plan is old and proven.

Evolve new species, better suited to the new environment.

That starts with taking one idea seriously: human civilization now evolves through its technologies, and each of us earns our place within that process.

This book won't hand you simple answers. It's written for someone who wants a decent paycheck for an honest day's work, and who would happily learn a new skill to get it. Someone who'd compromise if it fixed anything, drop an old position if a better one came along, and doesn't want a handout or a bailout. Someone who keeps getting told to pick a side, when neither side is talking about the thing actually squeezing them.

If that sounds like you, you're not confused, and you're not alone. You're just missing a name for what's happening.

Whether you're in Gen Z hoping to start a family or a retiree hoping to see grandkids, this book will show you how life leaves a bygone age behind and evolves, on purpose, into the environment you actually live in.

You'll get something Maria never had: a clear view of what's coming, and what you can do about it.

## Book Series

OPTION ONE:

_Techvolution: On the Origin of Technological Species_ presents a different way of understanding human civilization, and its ideas aren't meant to stay on the blackboard. We'll learn why our fate is driven not by our beliefs or ideologies, but by the technologies we create and use. Then we can put that knowledge to work and live better lives.

**Book III—Bumblebee Economics:** A better life starts with a better day. Rich or poor, old or young, everyone needs food, water, and shelter every day. To get them we need to create power, transfer energy, make products, and distribute them. So how do we make that working day more consistent and reliable?

We don't need to invent the answer. Nature already has. Every plant can grow because every plant uses the same sunlight. Predators eat most meats. Decomposers break down any lifeform. A bee works any flower it finds. When the inputs are universal, everyone gets to play.

Practically speaking, this means we need far more standards across our economy. We're good at making standards like USB, shipping containers, and VIN numbers. Less good at settling on them: the DVD's successor foolishly split into two rival discs. Worse, we have few standards for selling, staffing, or moving goods and people. Just think of the pains of resume writing, interviews, onboarding, and learning the ropes of a new job, never mind a new industry.

But with coherent standards, Maria's next niche is easier to find, and her knowledge and skill sets carry straight into a new part of the ecosystem. Book III details the overarching adaptations that enforce widespread standards and interoperability in nature. When bees, flowers, and every other organism are put to constant use, they seem to thrive on it, and nothing is wasted. Happily, bounty is the result in nature, and in civilization too.

**Book II—Mixed Ideological Politics:** That better working day has a huge obstacle: our stories. We see a heat pump and think it's "green," or see a diesel truck drive by and think it's "red-blooded American." Then we reject good tools for wearing the wrong label. Labels do to tools what rival discs did to the DVD's successor. They split the users, nobody gets the whole toolbox, and the whole ecosystem grows slower, if at all.

Nature doesn't function that way. Forests grow where the sun shines and the water runs. Solar panels and diesel generators should go wherever they can contribute to a healthy ecosystem, without anyone feeling they've betrayed a tribe. Book II teaches us how to strip story-based identities from our tools so we can respond to real problems as quickly as nature does. That freedom to grow or shed tools as we find our niche is political moderation reimagined for the Internet Age.

**Book I—A New Identity:** A better working day and the freedom to choose our tools both rest on something underneath. Book II strips the labels off our tools. Book I strips them off ourselves. We have names for our nationality, our ethnicity, our job, our politics, but no name for the identity that quietly runs our lives: the tools we use to house ourselves, get around, and earn a living. Because we miss it, we blame ourselves, the other party, or bad luck when the ecosystem shifts under our feet, as it's shifting right now with AI.

Evolution never saw Maria as a regional manager or a college graduate. It saw someone selling physical-media stories in the Internet Age, and slow adaptations get replaced, everywhere and in every era, regardless of the stories we tell about them. But someone who knows this can read the wind instead of cursing it, shed the tool, and keep the knowledge. Maria's eye for a good story and her grip on logistics are needed in whatever comes next.

That recognition is where everything else begins. It's the book in your hands.
